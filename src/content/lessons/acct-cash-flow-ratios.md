## The three sections of the cash flow statement

Net income and cash are different because accrual accounting records revenue and expenses when earned and incurred. The statement of cash flows explains the change in cash in three sections. Operating activities come from the main business. Investing activities buy and sell long-term assets and investments. Financing activities raise or repay money from owners and lenders.

Under U.S. GAAP, interest paid and interest and dividends received are operating cash flows, while dividends paid are financing. The indirect method starts with net income and adjusts it to cash. It is the method most companies use.

| Item | Section | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cash received from customers | Operating | Inflow |
| Wages and supplier payments | Operating | Outflow |
| Purchase of equipment (capital expenditure) | Investing | Outflow |
| Sale of a building | Investing | Inflow |
| Issuing stock or borrowing | Financing | Inflow |
| Repaying loan principal or paying dividends | Financing | Outflow |

## Work the indirect method

Add back expenses that used no cash, such as depreciation. Subtract gains on asset sales because the full sale proceeds belong in investing. For current operating assets, an increase means cash was tied up, so subtract it. For current operating liabilities, an increase means the company kept cash, so add it.

> **Example**
>
> Northgate Supply reports net income of $50,000 and depreciation of $12,000. It sold equipment for $10,000 cash at a $4,000 gain. Accounts receivable rose $8,000, inventory fell $5,000, accounts payable rose $3,000, and accrued liabilities fell $2,000. It bought new equipment for $30,000, issued stock for $15,000, repaid $12,000 of a loan, and paid $9,000 in dividends. Beginning cash was $18,000.
>
> 1. Operating: 50,000 + 12,000 - 4,000 - 8,000 + 5,000 + 3,000 - 2,000 = 56,000.
> 2. Investing: -30,000 + 10,000 = -20,000.
> 3. Financing: +15,000 - 12,000 - 9,000 = -6,000.
> 4. Net change in cash: 56,000 - 20,000 - 6,000 = +30,000. Ending cash: 18,000 + 30,000 = 48,000.
>
> Ending cash is $48,000. Free cash flow, a common measure of cash available after keeping the business running, is operating cash flow minus capital expenditures: 56,000 - 30,000 = $26,000.

## Calculate the core ratios

Use this fictional data for every ratio below. Revenue is $400,000, cost of goods sold $240,000, operating income $60,000, interest expense $6,000, and net income $40,000. Current assets are $120,000 (cash $30,000, receivables $40,000, inventory $50,000). Current liabilities are $80,000. Total liabilities are $200,000 and equity is $300,000. Averages for the year are inventory $48,000, receivables $38,000, total assets $480,000, and equity $290,000.

| Ratio | Formula | Result | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gross margin | Gross profit / revenue | 160,000 / 400,000 = 40% | Share of each sales dollar left after product cost |
| Operating margin | Operating income / revenue | 15% | Profit from operations before interest and taxes |
| Net margin | Net income / revenue | 10% | Profit kept from each sales dollar |
| Return on assets | Net income / average total assets | 8.3% | Profit generated by the assets |
| Return on equity | Net income / average equity | 13.8% | Profit earned on the owners' investment |
| Current ratio | Current assets / current liabilities | 1.50 | Ability to pay short-term debts |
| Quick ratio | (Cash + receivables + short-term investments) / current liabilities | 70,000 / 80,000 = 0.88 | Short-term ability without selling inventory |
| Inventory turnover | COGS / average inventory | 5.0 times; about 73 days | How quickly inventory sells |
| Receivables turnover | Revenue (credit sales) / average receivables | 10.5 times; about 35 days | How quickly customers pay |
| Asset turnover | Revenue / average total assets | 0.83 | Sales produced per dollar of assets |
| Debt-to-equity | Total liabilities / total equity | 0.67 | How much the company relies on borrowing |
| Times interest earned | Operating income / interest expense | 10.0 times | Cushion for paying interest |

- The DuPont model splits return on equity: net margin x asset turnover x equity multiplier. Here 0.10 x 0.833 x (480,000 / 290,000 = 1.655) = 13.8%.
- Horizontal analysis compares a line across years: revenue rising from $360,000 to $400,000 is an 11.1% increase. Vertical analysis states each line as a percent of a base, such as cost of goods sold equal to 60% of revenue.

## Interpret instead of only calculating

A ratio means little alone. Compare it with the company's prior years, with close competitors, and with industry figures. A current ratio of 1.5 may be comfortable for a manufacturer and unnecessary for a grocery chain that collects cash daily. A falling inventory turnover could mean weaker demand or a deliberate build-up before a busy season. Read the notes and MD&A to learn which explanation is true.

State conclusions with evidence: name the ratio, the change, the likely cause, and what you would check next. Avoid saying a company is healthy or weak based on one number.

> **Try it yourself**
>
> A company has current assets of $90,000 including $25,000 of inventory, current liabilities of $60,000, net income of $18,000, revenue of $240,000, and average equity of $150,000. Calculate the current ratio, quick ratio (assume all other current assets are cash and receivables), net margin, and return on equity. Then write one sentence interpreting the quick ratio.

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

Current ratio: 90,000 / 60,000 = 1.50. Quick ratio: (90,000 - 25,000) / 60,000 = 1.08. Net margin: 18,000 / 240,000 = 7.5%. ROE: 18,000 / 150,000 = 12%. Interpretation: the company could pay its current liabilities from cash and receivables alone, without relying on selling inventory, though the result should be compared with prior years and the industry.

</details>

## Key terms

**Indirect method.** A way of reporting operating cash flow that starts with net income and adjusts for noncash items and working capital changes.

**Free cash flow.** Operating cash flow minus capital expenditures.

**Quick ratio.** Cash, short-term investments, and receivables divided by current liabilities.

**Return on equity.** Net income divided by average shareholders' equity.

**Horizontal analysis.** Comparing financial statement amounts across periods, usually as percent changes.
