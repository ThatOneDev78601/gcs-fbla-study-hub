## Cost-volume-profit analysis

Managerial accounting produces information for decisions inside the business. It starts with cost behavior. Variable costs change in total with volume, such as materials. Fixed costs stay the same in total within a relevant range, such as rent. Contribution margin is price minus variable cost per unit. It is the amount each unit contributes toward fixed costs and then profit.

> **Example**
>
> A fictional water-bottle maker sells bottles for $50. Variable cost is $30 per bottle and fixed costs are $60,000 a year. Expected sales are 3,600 bottles.
>
> 1. Contribution margin per unit: 50 - 30 = $20. Contribution margin ratio: 20 / 50 = 40%.
> 2. Break-even units: 60,000 / 20 = 3,000 bottles. Break-even sales: 60,000 / 0.40 = $150,000.
> 3. Units for a $20,000 target profit: (60,000 + 20,000) / 20 = 4,000 bottles.
> 4. Margin of safety: (3,600 - 3,000) / 3,600 = 16.7% of expected sales.
>
> The company breaks even at 3,000 bottles and has a 16.7% cushion at expected volume. If fixed costs rose, break-even would rise; if price rose, contribution margin and profit per unit would rise.

## Use relevant costs for decisions

A relevant cost differs between the alternatives and occurs in the future. Sunk costs, which were already spent, are never relevant. Fixed costs that continue no matter what is chosen are also not relevant. Opportunity cost, which is the benefit lost by not choosing the next best option, is relevant even though it never appears in the accounting records.

Marginal analysis applies the same idea one unit at a time. Keep producing as long as the revenue from one more unit is greater than the cost of that unit.

| Decision | Facts | Relevant comparison | Choice |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Special order | Idle capacity; customer offers $38 for 500 units; variable cost $30 plus $2 special shipping | Incremental revenue 19,000 minus incremental cost 16,000 | Accept: +$3,000, if regular prices are not affected |
| Make or buy | Make: $12 variable plus $2 of avoidable fixed cost per unit. Allocated fixed cost of $4 remains either way. Buy: $15 | Make $14 versus buy $15 | Make, unless the freed space could earn more than $1 per unit |
| Keep or drop a product | Product line loses $5,000 after $12,000 of allocated fixed costs that would remain | Lost contribution margin of $7,000 versus no savings | Keep the line; dropping it lowers total profit by $7,000 |

## Apply overhead and calculate variances

A predetermined overhead rate assigns indirect costs to products. If estimated overhead is $240,000 and the company expects 12,000 direct labor hours, the rate is $20 per hour. A job using 30 hours is charged $600 of overhead. At year end, the difference between actual and applied overhead is underapplied (actual higher) or overapplied (actual lower). Job order costing tracks costs by individual job; process costing averages costs across identical units.

A standard cost is what a unit should cost. A variance is the difference between actual and standard. A favorable variance (F) means actual cost was lower; unfavorable (U) means it was higher. Always find the standard quantity allowed for the actual output first.

> **Example**
>
> Each unit should use 2 pounds of material at $4 a pound and 0.5 labor hours at $20 an hour. The company made 1,000 units, using 2,100 pounds bought at $3.90 and 520 labor hours paid at $21.
>
> 1. Standard quantities allowed: 1,000 x 2 = 2,000 pounds; 1,000 x 0.5 = 500 hours.
> 2. Material price variance: (3.90 - 4.00) x 2,100 = $210 F.
> 3. Material quantity variance: (2,100 - 2,000) x 4.00 = $400 U. Total material variance: $190 U (actual 8,190 versus standard 8,000).
> 4. Labor rate variance: (21 - 20) x 520 = $520 U.
> 5. Labor efficiency variance: (520 - 500) x 20 = $400 U. Total labor variance: $920 U (actual 10,920 versus standard 10,000).
>
> Cheaper material may have been lower quality, which could explain the extra pounds used. Investigate variances as connected clues rather than blaming one department automatically.

## Internal controls and audit cases

Internal controls protect assets and make records reliable. The fraud triangle explains why fraud happens: pressure, opportunity, and rationalization. Controls mainly reduce opportunity.

Use the control principles to fix each case: establish responsibility, separate duties, document transactions, protect physical assets, verify independently, and rotate duties or require vacations.

| Scenario | Weakness | Control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One clerk receives customer checks, records payments, and reconciles the bank account | Lapping: a clerk can steal one customer's payment and cover it with the next | Separate cash handling from recording and reconciliation |
| Payroll includes a worker no supervisor recognizes | Ghost employee | HR adds employees; payroll pays them; supervisors approve hours |
| The petty cash box has no receipts for $140 of spending | No documentation | Require receipts and an imprest fund replenished only for documented spending |
| The same employee has never taken a vacation in six years | Concealed errors or fraud may continue | Mandatory vacations and job rotation |

> **Try it yourself**
>
> A product should use 3 hours of labor at $18 an hour. The company made 400 units using 1,150 hours paid at $18.50. Calculate the labor rate and efficiency variances and say whether each is favorable or unfavorable.

## Key terms

**Contribution margin.** Selling price minus variable cost per unit, available to cover fixed costs and profit.

**Break-even point.** The sales level at which total revenue equals total cost and profit is zero.

**Relevant cost.** A future cost that differs between alternatives.

**Sunk cost.** A cost already incurred that cannot be changed by any decision.

**Standard cost.** The expected cost of producing one unit under normal conditions.

**Segregation of duties.** Assigning authorization, custody, and recordkeeping to different people.
