## Equipment and chemical safety

Agriculture is one of the most dangerous industries. Tractor rollovers are a leading cause of farm deaths, so tractors should have rollover protective structures (ROPS) and riders should wear seat belts. Power take-off (PTO) shafts can entangle clothing in a fraction of a second; keep shields in place and never step over a running shaft. Before servicing equipment, shut it off, remove the key, and use lockout/tagout procedures. Slow-moving vehicle emblems are required on equipment traveling on public roads. Grain bins can trap and suffocate workers; never enter a bin with flowing grain or without a trained partner and lockout.

Read the procedure, the operator's manual, and the pesticide label before work. The label is the law: it states the protective equipment required, the restricted-entry interval before workers may re-enter a treated field, and storage and disposal rules. The EPA's Worker Protection Standard requires training and protections for agricultural workers. Safety data sheets describe chemical hazards.

1. Emergency response: make the scene safe (shut off equipment and power), call 911 with the exact location, give first aid within your training, and do not move an injured person unless there is immediate danger.
2. Report: notify the supervisor and complete an accident report with date, time, location, people involved, equipment, what happened, injuries, witnesses, and immediate causes.
3. Compliance: report serious injuries to OSHA where required, keep records, and follow insurer notice requirements.
4. Prevent: investigate root causes and change procedures, training, or equipment.

## Budgets, records, and break-even

An enterprise budget estimates revenue and costs for one product, such as an acre of corn. Variable costs (seed, fertilizer, chemicals, fuel, custom hire) change with the acres planted. Fixed or overhead costs (land rent or ownership costs, machinery depreciation, insurance, and management) are spread across the enterprise. Records support taxes (farm income is reported on Schedule F), loans, insurance claims, and better decisions.

- Seasonal risk: most expenses come in spring while revenue comes after harvest, so farms often use an operating line of credit and plan cash flow month by month.
- Federal crop insurance, administered by the USDA Risk Management Agency, offers yield protection and revenue protection policies. Farms also insure buildings, equipment, livestock, and liability.

> **Example**
>
> A corn enterprise has variable costs of $450 per acre and overhead of $200 per acre. Expected yield is 180 bushels per acre, and the expected price is $4.20 per bushel.
>
> 1. Total cost per acre: 450 + 200 = $650.
> 2. Break-even price: 650 / 180 = $3.61 per bushel.
> 3. Break-even yield at $4.20: 650 / 4.20 = 155 bushels per acre.
> 4. Expected profit: 180 x 4.20 - 650 = 756 - 650 = $106 per acre.
>
> The farm profits if price stays above $3.61 at expected yield or yield stays above 155 bushels at expected price. These numbers guide marketing decisions and how much risk to hedge.

## Marketing agricultural products

Commodities such as corn, soybeans, and wheat are largely undifferentiated, so farmers are price takers. Marketing choices include selling at harvest for the cash price, storing and selling later, forward contracts that set a price with a local buyer for future delivery, and hedging with futures contracts. Basis is the local cash price minus the futures price. If December corn futures are $4.50 and the local elevator pays $4.20, the basis is -$0.30, reflecting transportation and local supply and demand.

Value-added and direct marketing, such as farmers markets, community-supported agriculture subscriptions, organic certification, or on-farm processing, let farmers differentiate and earn more per unit with more labor and marketing work. Buyers include processors, grain elevators, food companies, retailers, restaurants, and consumers, each with different quality and volume needs. Global trade matters: a large share of some U.S. crops is exported, so tariffs, exchange rates, and foreign demand affect local prices.

## Ownership, contracts, government, and environment

Most U.S. farms are family owned, often as sole proprietorships, partnerships, family corporations, or LLCs. Cooperatives let farmers jointly buy supplies or market products. Land is often rented: a cash rent lease sets a fixed payment, while a crop share lease splits the crop and some costs between owner and tenant, which shares risk. Production contracts, common in poultry, specify what the grower provides and how they are paid.

Government involvement includes the USDA (research, inspection, conservation, and farm programs), farm bill programs for commodities, conservation, crop insurance, and nutrition, and Farm Service Agency loans for producers who cannot get commercial credit. Environmental obligations include Clean Water Act requirements, pesticide rules, and, in Maryland, nutrient management planning required for many farms to protect the Chesapeake Bay. Ethical issues include animal welfare, worker safety, honest labeling, and stewardship of land and water.

## Natural systems, technology, and inventory

Agriculture depends on natural cycles: the water cycle supplies moisture; the nitrogen cycle supplies a key nutrient through soil organisms, legumes, and fertilizer; and the carbon cycle links photosynthesis and soil organic matter. Weather, soil health, pests, and seasons limit what can be done and when.

Production technology includes GPS-guided equipment, variable-rate application of seed and fertilizer based on soil maps, yield monitors, drones and satellite imagery for crop scouting, irrigation systems, improved seed genetics, and farm management software. Inventory management tracks grain in storage (quantity and quality), feed, fertilizer and chemicals, and livestock, and links to marketing decisions.

## Key terms

**ROPS.** Rollover protective structure that protects a tractor operator in a rollover.

**Restricted-entry interval.** The time after a pesticide application before workers may enter the field without protection.

**Enterprise budget.** A budget of revenue and costs for one farm product or activity.

**Basis.** Local cash price minus the futures price.

**Crop share lease.** A land rental agreement that splits the crop and some costs between owner and tenant.

**Precision agriculture.** Using GPS, sensors, and data to manage fields in small zones.
