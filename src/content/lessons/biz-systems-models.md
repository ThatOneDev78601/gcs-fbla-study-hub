## Economic systems and private enterprise

Every economy answers what to produce, how to produce it, and for whom. A traditional economy relies on custom. A command economy relies on government planning. A market economy relies on individual choices and prices. Most countries, including the United States, have mixed economies that combine markets with some government role.

The U.S. private enterprise system rests on private property, the profit motive, competition, and freedom of choice. Profit rewards businesses for taking risk and meeting needs. Competition pushes businesses to improve quality and lower prices. Risk is the possibility of loss. Economic risks come from changes in the economy or competition, natural risks from weather and disasters, and human risks from theft, errors, or poor decisions.

| Market structure | Number of sellers | Product | Price control | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Perfect competition | Many | Identical | None | Wheat farming |
| Monopolistic competition | Many | Differentiated | Some | Restaurants |
| Oligopoly | Few | Similar or differentiated | Considerable; firms watch each other | Airlines, wireless carriers |
| Monopoly | One | Unique, no close substitutes | Significant, often regulated | A local water utility |

## Forms of ownership

A franchise is a licensing arrangement, not a separate legal form. The franchisee buys the right to use the franchisor's name, products, and system, pays an initial fee and ongoing royalties, and follows the franchisor's rules. The franchisee gets a proven model and loses some independence.

| Form | Owner liability | Taxation | Best fit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sole proprietorship | Unlimited personal liability | Owner's personal return | One owner, low startup cost |
| General partnership | Unlimited, shared, and each partner can bind the others | Passed through to partners | Two or more owners sharing management |
| Limited partnership | General partners unlimited; limited partners limited to their investment | Passed through | Investors who do not manage |
| C corporation | Limited to investment | Corporation taxed; dividends taxed again (double taxation) | Raising money from many investors |
| S corporation | Limited | Passed through; limits on number and type of shareholders | Small corporations avoiding double tax |
| Limited liability company (LLC) | Limited | Flexible; usually passed through | Small businesses wanting protection and simplicity |
| Cooperative | Limited | Special rules | Members pooling buying or selling power |

## How the business functions connect

Each business function depends on the others. A decision in one creates work in the rest. Tests often describe a task and ask which function performs it.

| Function | Main job | Connects to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Management | Plans, organizes, staffs, leads, and controls | Sets goals for every other function |
| Marketing | Learns customer needs; sets product, price, place, and promotion | Gives sales forecasts to production and finance |
| Production and operations | Turns inputs into goods or services efficiently | Needs staff from HR and money from finance |
| Finance | Raises and manages money; budgets | Funds every function |
| Accounting | Records and reports financial results | Supplies data to management and investors |
| Human resources | Recruits, trains, pays, and supports employees | Staffs every function |
| Information management | Collects, stores, and protects data and systems | Supports decisions in every function |

> **Example**
>
> A small bicycle shop decides to add electric bikes. Trace the effects across functions.
>
> Marketing researches demand and sets prices. Finance arranges money for inventory and checks the cash flow effect. Operations finds suppliers and adds a charging and repair area. HR trains mechanics on batteries and safety. Accounting tracks the new product line's margin. Information management adds the bikes to the point-of-sale and inventory system. Management sets the goal and reviews results.

## Business models and organizational design

A business model explains how a company creates value for customers and earns money. A subscription model charges a recurring fee, such as a meal kit service. A freemium model gives basic features free and charges for upgrades. A marketplace or platform connects buyers and sellers and takes a fee. An advertising model offers free content and sells audience attention. A licensing model charges others to use intellectual property.

Organizational design is the structure of jobs and reporting lines. A functional structure groups people by specialty, such as marketing or finance. A divisional structure groups by product, customer, or region. A matrix structure gives employees two bosses, a functional manager and a project manager. A flat structure has few management levels and wide spans of control, which speeds decisions but stretches managers.

Businesses must adapt when markets change. Signs that change is needed include falling sales, new competitors, new technology, and shifting customer habits. Adaptation can mean new products, new channels, or a new model, such as a video rental chain moving to streaming.

> **Try it yourself**
>
> Two friends will open a food truck. One will run it; the other will invest $20,000 but not work there. They want to protect their personal savings from lawsuits. Recommend a form of ownership and explain why two alternatives fit less well.

An LLC fits: both owners get limited liability, profits usually pass through to their personal returns, and the operating agreement can give the managing owner control while the investor's role stays limited. A general partnership would expose both to unlimited liability. A C corporation offers protection but adds double taxation and more formalities than a single food truck needs.

## Key terms

**Mixed economy.** An economy that combines market decisions with some government planning and regulation.

**Oligopoly.** A market with a few large sellers who watch and react to each other.

**Limited liability.** Owners can lose only what they invested in the business.

**Double taxation.** Corporate profits taxed once at the corporate level and again when paid as dividends.

**Business model.** How a company creates value for customers and earns revenue.

**Matrix structure.** An organization in which employees report to both a functional and a project manager.
