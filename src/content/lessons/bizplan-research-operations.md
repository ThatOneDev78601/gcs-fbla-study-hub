## Industry, market, and competitor research

A business plan persuades a reader that the idea is sound, so every claim needs evidence. Industry research describes the industry's size, growth, trends, and regulations, using sources such as the Census Bureau's business data, the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the SBA, trade associations, and reputable news. Record each fact with its source and date.

Market research defines the target customer and estimates demand. Total addressable market (TAM) is everyone who could buy the product; serviceable available market (SAM) is the part the business can reach; serviceable obtainable market (SOM) is the realistic share it can win. Build estimates from the bottom up when possible: number of likely customers times purchases per year times price. Support assumptions with primary research such as surveys, interviews, or a small test.

Competitor analysis compares direct competitors (same product), indirect competitors (different product, same need), and substitutes. A comparison table of price, quality, location, service, and weaknesses shows the gap the business will fill. Conclude each research section with what it means for the business, not only what the data says.

> **Example**
>
> A fictional phone repair kiosk in a mall estimates its market from the bottom up.
>
> 1. The county has about 120,000 smartphone owners within a 15-minute drive (from population data and a published ownership rate).
> 2. Assume 10% need a repair each year: 12,000 repairs (cite the source of the rate, or label it an assumption tested by a survey).
> 3. Three competitors already serve the area. Assume the kiosk can win 8% of repairs in year one: 960 repairs, or about 80 a month.
> 4. At an average price of $80, year-one revenue is about $76,800.
>
> Each step is traceable to a source or a labeled assumption, so a reader can check it. The financial model then uses 80 repairs a month as its steady-state volume, which keeps the research and the numbers consistent.

## Operations, legal structure, and people

The operations section explains how the business will work day to day: location and layout, equipment, suppliers, production or service process, quality control, hours, and technology. State realistic constraints, such as lease terms, supplier lead times, or how many repairs one technician can complete in a day.

Explain the chosen legal structure and why it fits, plus required licenses, permits, insurance, and intellectual property protection. Staffing covers the roles needed, when each will be hired, pay, and how the owner's time is used. Compensation should match local wage data. Advisers, such as an accountant, attorney, mentor, or board, strengthen a plan when their roles are specific.

## Growth, risks, milestones, and appendices

Long-term development explains how the business will grow: new locations, products, or channels, and what must be true first. A risk section names the main risks (lower demand, a new competitor, a key supplier failing, the owner's illness) and the plan for each. Milestones give dates and measures: opening day, break-even month, second technician hired.

Supporting documents, such as resumes, quotes from suppliers, a lease summary, survey results, and detailed financial statements, go in appendices referenced in the text. Follow the event's required report sequence, section names, page limits, and formatting. A plan that is strong but out of order or over the limit can lose points.

## Key terms

**Total addressable market.** The total demand for a product if every possible customer bought it.

**Bottom-up estimate.** A market estimate built from customers, purchase frequency, and price.

**Indirect competitor.** A business meeting the same need with a different product.

**Milestone.** A dated, measurable goal that marks progress in a plan.

**Appendix.** Supporting material placed after the main report and referenced in it.
