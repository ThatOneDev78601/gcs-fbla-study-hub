## Choose stories and build the rundown

A newscast's producer chooses stories using news values (timeliness, proximity, impact, prominence, conflict, human interest) plus what the audience can see and hear. A strong broadcast story has visuals and sound, not just facts. Check each story's sources as carefully as in print.

The rundown is the newscast's plan. Each row lists the order, a one- or two-word slug that names the story, who reads it, the format, and its running time. Formats include a reader (anchor reads with no video), VO (voice-over: anchor reads over video), SOT (sound on tape: a clip of someone speaking), VO/SOT (video then a sound bite), and PKG (a reporter's packaged story with narration, sound bites, and a stand-up).

## Write for the ear

Broadcast copy is written to be heard once. Use short sentences, one idea each, in active voice and present or present perfect tense ("The bakery reopens today"). Put attribution first ("Police say..."). Round numbers and spell out what the anchor should say: "about one-point-two million dollars." Write phonetic spellings for hard names. Avoid quotation marks and abbreviations the anchor might stumble on.

Most anchors read about 150 words per minute, or about 3 words per second. A 20-second reader is about 60 words. Time scripts by reading them aloud with a stopwatch.

Scripts often use a two-column format: video instructions on the left (camera shots, graphics, lower-third names, clip in and out times) and the audio on the right (what the anchor reads and the sound bite's first and last words, called the in-cue and out-cue).

> **Example**
>
> Write a 20-second VO for a fictional story: a local bakery closed by a kitchen fire in June reopens today with a new oven and ten new jobs.
>
> VIDEO: Exterior of bakery; customers in line; baker pulling bread from new oven. AUDIO: "A Main Street favorite is back in business. Riverside Bakery reopens this morning, five months after a kitchen fire closed its doors. The owners installed a new oven and hired ten more workers. Customers lined up before sunrise for the first loaves." That is about 50 words, roughly 17 to 20 seconds when read naturally.

## Interviews, B-roll, and production

Plan each interview for usable sound bites: ask open questions that invite short, vivid answers, and ask the subject to say their name and title spelled out on camera. Frame interviewees slightly off-center, looking toward the reporter, with the camera at eye level. Record in a quiet place and use a lavalier or handheld microphone close to the speaker.

B-roll is supporting video that covers narration: wide, medium, and close shots of the people and places in the story, each held 8 to 10 seconds. Natural sound (nat sound), such as an oven door closing, makes stories feel real. A stand-up is the reporter speaking on camera at the scene, often as a bridge in the middle of a package. Lower-third graphics identify speakers. Captions make the segment accessible, and credits list everyone who contributed and any licensed material.

## Document the process and present as a team

Keep a production log: story pitches and why each was chosen or dropped, the verification log, script drafts with edits and reasons, equipment and software used, and problems solved. This record lets the team explain editorial choices and shows the work is original.

For a segment showcase, assign clear roles (producer, anchors, reporter, editor), rehearse handoffs, test playback on the event's equipment or a backup device, and prepare for judges' questions about why stories were chosen, how facts were checked, and what the team would change. Confirm length limits, file format, and other requirements in the current event guide.

## Key terms

**Rundown.** The ordered plan of a newscast listing each story's format, talent, and time.

**VO/SOT.** A story format with anchor voice-over followed by a sound bite.

**B-roll.** Supporting video shown while narration or an anchor's voice plays.

**Package.** A complete reporter story with narration, sound bites, and often a stand-up.

**Out-cue.** The last words of a sound bite, written in the script so the anchor knows when it ends.
