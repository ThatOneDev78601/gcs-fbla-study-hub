## Connect the business functions

A business combines resources to solve customer problems. Revenue is earned sales; profit is revenue less expenses. Manufacturers transform inputs, wholesalers mainly sell to organizations, retailers mainly sell to consumers, and service providers perform work. Marketing communicates value, operations delivers it, finance plans money, and human resources supports people. A promotion fails if operations cannot fulfill its promise.

## Compare ownership

Sole proprietorships generally do not separate owners from business liabilities. Partnerships require agreements about decisions, contributions, profit, and exit. Corporations are separate entities with shares and governance. LLCs can limit personal exposure under applicable rules, while tax treatment is a separate question. A franchise is a contractual operating model rather than one universal ownership form. Limited liability does not eliminate every personal obligation.

Organizational design also matters. Functional structures group people by specialties, divisional structures group by product or region, and matrix structures combine reporting dimensions. Centralization concentrates decisions, while decentralization distributes authority. A wide span of control gives one manager more direct reports. None is always best: task complexity, skills, coordination needs, and scale affect the choice.

## Manage workflow and quality

Productivity compares outputs with inputs. Quality control detects defects; quality assurance builds processes to prevent them. Supplier reliability, inventory, capacity, and safety affect delivery. A bottleneck limits throughput even if other stations are faster. Standard operating procedures clarify repeatable work, but exceptions still need a responsible decision-maker. Distinguish fixed costs from activity-driven variable costs before changing a production plan.

Nonprofit organizations can earn revenue and employ staff; they apply resources to their mission rather than distributing profit as owners’ returns. A business model, such as subscription, transaction sales, or a service fee, explains how the organization creates and captures value.

> **Example**
>
> Packaging handles 30 kits per hour; checkout handles only 18.
>
> Checkout constrains completed sales to 18 per hour. Faster packaging alone cannot increase throughput; a trained checkout helper might.

## Key terms

**Bottleneck.** A step limiting total process output.

**Franchise.** A contract granting use of a brand and operating system.
