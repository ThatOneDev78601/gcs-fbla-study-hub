## Research the career and the position

Start with reliable sources. The Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook gives each occupation's duties, typical education, median pay, and projected job growth. O*NET lists the skills and tasks of each occupation. The employer's own website and job posting show what this organization values. Record each fact with its source and date, because pay and outlook figures change every year.

Choose a position that fits your actual skills and experience. An entry-level role you can explain with real examples is a stronger application than a senior role you cannot support. Read the posting and list its required and preferred qualifications. Those become the checklist for your resume, letter, and interview answers.

## Write a targeted resume

A resume for a high school student is usually one page. Typical sections are contact information, education, experience (jobs, internships, and volunteer work), leadership and activities, skills, and awards. A chronological format lists the most recent experience first and suits most students. Use standard headings and plain formatting so applicant tracking software can read it.

Each experience bullet should start with a strong action verb, describe what you did, and show a result or scale when you can truthfully state one. Tailor the order and wording to the posting: put the most relevant experience first and use the employer's terms for skills you really have.

| Weak bullet | Stronger bullet | What changed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Responsible for social media | Wrote and scheduled 3 posts a week for the chapter Instagram, growing followers from 180 to 310 over the school year | Action verb, specific task, measured result |
| Helped at the food drive | Organized 12 volunteers into sorting shifts for a two-day food drive that collected 1,400 items | Shows leadership and scale |
| Worked cash register | Processed 80 to 120 customer transactions per shift and balanced the drawer at close | Shows accuracy and responsibility |

- Never invent or inflate experience, titles, or numbers. If you do not know a figure, describe the task without one.
- Proofread names, dates, and contact information. A typo in your own email address can cost the interview.

## Write the cover letter and follow the rules

A cover letter connects you to one specific position. The opening names the position and why you are interested in this employer. The body matches two or three of the posting's key requirements to specific evidence from your experience. The closing restates interest, requests an interview, and thanks the reader. Use business letter format and keep it to one page.

Competitive events add their own rules. Before submitting, check the current guideline for the number and type of files, page limits, the required addressee or position, file format, and anything prohibited, such as photographs or personal information beyond what is requested. A strong application that breaks a stated rule can lose points or be disqualified.

> **Example**
>
> A posting for a summer camp office assistant asks for organization, phone skills, and spreadsheet experience. Write the body paragraph's core.
>
> "As treasurer of my school's FBLA chapter, I track dues and fundraising for 45 members in a shared spreadsheet and reconcile it with the adviser each month. I also answer phones and greet customers during weekend shifts at a local garden center, where I take messages and direct questions to the right staff member." Each sentence matches a requirement to real evidence.

## Prepare for the interview

Research the employer's products, customers, and values, and prepare two or three thoughtful questions to ask, such as how success is measured in the first three months. Practice common questions aloud: tell me about yourself, why this position, a strength and a weakness, a challenge you overcame, a time you worked on a team, and where you see your career going.

Answer behavioral questions with the STAR method: situation, task, action, and result. Keep the situation short and spend most of the answer on what you did and what happened. Use a different true example for each question when possible.

Close by restating interest, asking about next steps, and thanking the interviewer. Send a brief thank-you message within a day. Dress, arrival time, eye contact, and a firm handshake shape first impressions, and the event's dress code and permitted materials apply in competition.

> **Example**
>
> Question: "Tell me about a time you solved a problem on a team."
>
> Situation: "Our chapter's fundraiser sign-up sheet kept double-booking volunteers." Task: "As events chair, I needed a fix before the next sale." Action: "I moved sign-ups to a shared form that closed each shift when full and sent reminders two days ahead." Result: "We had no double-bookings for the rest of the semester, and no-shows dropped from five to one."

> **Try it yourself**
>
> Write a STAR answer to "Describe a time you had to meet a tight deadline," using a real experience from school, work, or a club. Then check it: Is the situation under two sentences? Is the action specific to you? Is the result concrete?

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

A strong answer names one real situation briefly, states your responsibility, describes two or three specific steps you took (such as breaking the work into parts, asking for help early, or adjusting a schedule), and ends with a concrete, honest result such as finishing on time, the grade or feedback received, or what you would do differently. Weak answers describe what "we" did without your role, or end without a result.

## Key terms

**Occupational Outlook Handbook.** A Bureau of Labor Statistics guide to occupations' duties, education, pay, and outlook.

**Action verb.** A specific verb, such as organized or analyzed, that begins a resume bullet.

**Applicant tracking system.** Software employers use to collect and screen applications.

**STAR method.** Answering behavioral questions with situation, task, action, and result.

**Behavioral question.** An interview question asking how you handled a past situation.
