## Start from the career's requirements

A portfolio is an argument that you are preparing for a specific career. Begin with research: the career's typical duties, education and certification path, pay range, outlook, and challenges, each from a reliable, cited source. Then list the five to eight skills the career requires most, such as data analysis, client communication, or design software.

Build an evidence matrix with one row per required skill. For each skill, list the portfolio items that prove it and what each item shows. Rows with no evidence reveal gaps to fill with a class project, a volunteer role, or a certification, or to acknowledge honestly as a next step.

| Required skill (sample career: marketing analyst) | Evidence item | What it proves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Data analysis | Spreadsheet of survey results with charts from an economics project | Cleaned data, calculated averages, chose suitable charts |
| Written communication | Published article in the school newspaper | Clear writing for a general audience, met a deadline |
| Teamwork and leadership | Fundraiser plan and results summary as chapter events chair | Planned tasks, led volunteers, reported results |
| Software skills | Certificate of completion for a spreadsheet course | Verified training |
| Business knowledge | Gap: no evidence yet | Plan: take the marketing elective next semester |

## Explain your own contribution

Judges and employers want to know what you did. For group work, describe the team's goal briefly, then your specific role and decisions, using "I" for your work and "we" for shared work. For awards and certifications, explain what was required to earn them. For school activities and jobs, connect the experience to a skill in the career.

Never claim experience you do not have or present others' work as yours. A modest, well-explained item is stronger than an impressive one you cannot discuss.

> **Example**
>
> A student's portfolio includes a team business plan that won a local award. How should the student describe it?
>
> "Our four-person team wrote a business plan for a phone repair kiosk, which placed second at the regional competition. I built the financial section: I researched part costs from three suppliers, created the startup budget and 12-month cash flow, and presented the financials to the judges. That work is why I chose a career in financial analysis." The description credits the team and makes the student's role clear.

## Organize, protect, and test the portfolio

A well-organized portfolio lets a reader find evidence for any requirement in seconds. Privacy and access checks prevent a strong portfolio from failing on the day of presentation.

1. Organize by skill or by career requirement, with a table of contents or a home page that shows the structure.
2. Label every item with a title, date, and a one-sentence explanation of what it shows.
3. Remove private information: home address, student ID numbers, birth date, and other people's personal details. Get permission before including photos of other people or work that includes them.
4. For digital portfolios, test every link and file from a different device and an account that is not logged in. Check sharing permissions.
5. Keep visual aids simple: consistent fonts and colors, readable text, and one idea per slide or page.
6. Check the current event guidelines for format, page or file limits, and required sections.

## Present and answer questions

Open with the career you chose and why. Walk through the strongest evidence for the most important skills, using the rating sheet's categories as a guide so judges hear each scored element. Close with your next steps: education, certifications, or experience you plan to gain. Rehearse with a timer and practice handing over or displaying materials.

Expect questions such as why this career, which item you are proudest of, what you learned from a setback, and how you will close a gap. Answer with specific examples from the portfolio.

> **Try it yourself**
>
> Choose a career you are considering. Build a three-row evidence matrix: one required skill per row, one real item from your own experience, and what it proves. Mark any gap honestly.

## Key terms

**Career portfolio.** An organized collection of evidence showing preparation for a chosen career.

**Evidence matrix.** A table linking each required skill to the items that prove it.

**Artifact.** A single portfolio item, such as a project, certificate, or writing sample.

**Skill gap.** A required skill without evidence yet, paired with a plan to develop it.
