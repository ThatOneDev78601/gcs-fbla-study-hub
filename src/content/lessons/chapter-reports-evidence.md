## Confirm the rules first

The Community Service Project and the Local Chapter Annual Business Report are chapter events judged on a written report and a presentation. Their captured Maryland guides include dates from earlier seasons, so confirm the eligible activity period, school year, required sections, page limits, and categories with the current official guide and your adviser before writing season-specific instructions. Keep the confirmed rules in a checklist at the front of your working file.

Both reports reward the same habit: every claim in the report is backed by a record. Start keeping records at the first meeting, not the week before submission.

## Choose and justify a community project

A needs assessment finds what the community actually needs. Sources include partner organizations, local data, surveys, and interviews with people affected. Baseline evidence describes the situation before the project, such as the number of families on a food pantry's waiting list. Partner input matters: a shelter may need hygiene kits more than canned food. Document why you chose one project over others, using criteria such as need, chapter capacity, and measurable impact.

## Track participation accurately

Keep sign-in records for every activity with member names, roles, and hours. Count unique members, not total sign-ins: a member who attends five events is one participating member. The participation percentage equals unique participating members divided by total chapter membership.

> **Example**
>
> A chapter has 64 members. Sign-in sheets from eight events show 142 total sign-ins by 41 different members, totaling 356 volunteer hours.
>
> 1. Unique participation: 41 / 64 = 64.1% of members.
> 2. Average events per participating member: 142 / 41 = 3.5.
> 3. Average hours per participating member: 356 / 41 = 8.7.
>
> Report 64.1% participation, not 142 / 64 = 222%, which counts the same people repeatedly. Keep the sign-in sheets so a judge's question can be answered with records.

## Evaluate outcomes, publicity, and permissions

Outputs are what the chapter did, such as 600 hygiene kits assembled. Outcomes are what changed because of it, such as a shelter that could give a kit to every new resident for four months. Collect feedback from partners and participants, compare results with the baseline, and write recommendations for next year based on what worked and what did not.

Document publicity with copies of articles, social posts with reach figures, flyers, and announcements, each dated. Get written permission before using photos of people, especially minors and people served by the project, and protect their privacy. Credit sources and partners accurately.

## Connect the chapter year to the Program of Work

A Program of Work is the chapter's annual plan: goals, the activities that serve each goal, the person responsible, target dates, and how success will be measured. The annual business report shows how the year's activities carried out that plan. Organize evidence by the report categories in the current guide, which typically include areas such as career preparation, community service, partnerships, fundraising, publicity, and participation in state and national programs.

Include required elements such as the chapter president's remarks, written truthfully by the president. Use a consistent sequence, a table of contents, page numbers, and appendices for supporting documents, and count pages exactly as the guide defines them. In the presentation, walk through the year chronologically or by goal, showing how activities connected to goals and what the results were.

> **Try it yourself**
>
> A chapter of 80 members held a fundraiser (35 unique members), a service day (42 unique members), and a career talk (50 unique members). Across all three, 58 different members took part. (a) What is the chapter's overall participation rate? (b) Why can't you add the three event counts?

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

(a) 58 / 80 = 72.5%. (b) Adding 35 + 42 + 50 = 127 counts members who attended more than one event several times, which overstates participation. Unique members must be counted from the combined records.

</details>

## Key terms

**Needs assessment.** Research that identifies what a community actually needs before choosing a project.

**Baseline.** Evidence of conditions before a project begins, used to measure change.

**Unique participant.** A member counted once regardless of how many activities they joined.

**Output.** A direct product of an activity, such as items collected.

**Outcome.** The change that results from an activity, such as people served.

**Program of Work.** A chapter's annual plan of goals, activities, owners, dates, and measures.
