## Choose the approach before writing

Every business message starts with three questions: who will read it, what should they know or do afterward, and how will they likely react. The answers decide the order of the message.

Use the direct approach, with the main point first, for routine requests, good news, and information the reader expects. Use the indirect approach for bad news or a request the reader may resist: open with a neutral buffer, explain the reasons, state the decision clearly, and close with a helpful next step. Persuasive messages often follow AIDA: attention, interest, desire, and action.

| Message type | Approach | Opening sentence example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Routine request or inquiry | Direct | Could you send the updated price list for spring bulk orders by March 3? |
| Informational message | Direct | Starting Monday, the north parking lot will close for repaving until April 10. |
| Good news | Direct | Your application for the summer internship has been approved. |
| Bad news | Indirect | Thank you for your detailed proposal for the spring workshop series. |
| Persuasive request | Indirect (AIDA) | Last year, 40% of our volunteers left after their first event. |

## Write an effective email

A business email has a specific subject line, a greeting, the purpose in the first two sentences, supporting details in short paragraphs or a list, a clear action with a deadline, and a signature with contact information. Keep one main topic per email so it can be found and answered. Check the recipient list before sending, and use reply-all only when everyone needs the reply.

> **Example**
>
> Revise this weak email: "Subject: question. Hey, so I was wondering about the thing we talked about last week with the room, can you let me know whenever? Thanks."
>
> 1. Name the topic in the subject: "Room 214 booking for the March 12 workshop."
> 2. State the purpose first: "Could you confirm whether Room 214 is available on March 12 from 3 to 5 p.m.?"
> 3. Add needed details: expected attendance of 25 and the need for a projector.
> 4. Give the action and deadline: "Please reply by Friday so I can send invitations."
> 5. Close with a professional signature.
>
> The revised email lets the reader answer in one reply without searching for context. The deadline explains why a prompt answer matters.

## Letters and memos

Letters go to people outside the organization. In full block format, every line starts at the left margin. The parts, in order, are the letterhead or return address, date, inside address, salutation, body, complimentary close, signature block (handwritten signature above the typed name and title), and notations such as reference initials, "Enclosure," or "c:" for copies. In modified block format, the date, complimentary close, and signature block start at the center.

A memo is an internal document. Its heading lists TO, FROM, DATE, and SUBJECT, and it has no salutation or complimentary close. Many organizations now send memos as emails, but the same structure applies.

- Use "Dear Ms. Patel:" with a colon in business letters. A comma follows the salutation only in personal letters.
- Avoid "To whom it may concern" when you can find a name or a job title, such as "Dear Hiring Manager:".

## Bad news, persuasion, and summaries

A bad-news message must be clear enough that the reader is not confused about the decision, and kind enough to keep the relationship. Avoid blaming the reader and avoid false hope. Offer an alternative when one exists.

A summary keeps only the main ideas in the writer's own words, in roughly a quarter of the original length or less. An executive summary at the start of a report states the purpose, key findings, and recommendations so a busy reader can act without reading everything.

> **Example**
>
> A print shop must decline a nonprofit's request for 500 free flyers.
>
> Buffer: "Thank you for thinking of Brightline Print for your food drive." Reasons: "Our donation budget for this year was fully committed in January." Decision: "We are not able to donate the flyers this time." Alternative and goodwill: "We can print them at our 30% nonprofit rate, and we would be glad to be considered for next year's program, which opens in December."

## Reports

An informal report, such as a progress report, may be a memo of a few pages with headings. A formal report usually includes a title page, table of contents, executive summary, introduction (purpose, scope, and methods), findings, conclusions, recommendations, references, and appendices. Findings report what the evidence shows; conclusions explain what it means; recommendations say what to do. Keep these separate so readers can judge your reasoning.

Use descriptive headings, short paragraphs, and labeled tables and charts. Cite every source. Put detailed data in an appendix and refer to it in the text.

## Key terms

**Direct approach.** A message organization that states the main point first.

**Indirect approach.** A message organization that gives reasons before the main point, used for bad news or resistance.

**Full block format.** A letter layout with every line starting at the left margin.

**Buffer.** A neutral opening statement that begins a bad-news message.

**Executive summary.** A brief overview at the start of a report stating purpose, findings, and recommendations.
