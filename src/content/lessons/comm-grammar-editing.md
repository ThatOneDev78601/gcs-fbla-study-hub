## Agreement and pronouns

A verb agrees with its subject, not with a noun inside a phrase. "The box of files is on the desk" uses "is" because the subject is "box." Words such as each, everyone, either, and nobody are singular. With "neither ... nor" or "either ... or," the verb agrees with the closer subject: "Neither the manager nor the interns were told." Collective nouns such as team or committee usually take a singular verb in American English when the group acts as one.

Use the object form of a pronoun after a preposition: "between you and me," not "between you and I." Test a compound by removing the other person: "Send the report to me" shows that "to Sam and me" is correct. Use who for subjects and whom for objects: "Whom should I call?" because you would call him. A pronoun must agree with its antecedent and clearly refer to it.

| Incorrect | Correct | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The list of tasks are ready. | The list of tasks is ready. | Subject is "list" |
| Each of the employees have a badge. | Each of the employees has a badge. | "Each" is singular |
| Me and Jordan will present. | Jordan and I will present. | Subject form; name others first |
| The company raised it's prices. | The company raised its prices. | "It's" means "it is" |
| The policy will effect everyone. | The policy will affect everyone. | "Affect" is usually the verb; "effect" is usually the noun |

## Punctuation that changes meaning

Use a comma before a coordinating conjunction (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) that joins two independent clauses: "Sales rose, but costs rose faster." Use a comma after most introductory elements: "After the meeting, we will vote." Joining two independent clauses with only a comma creates a comma splice. Fix it with a period, a semicolon, or a comma and a conjunction.

A semicolon joins closely related independent clauses, and it comes before a transition such as however or therefore, with a comma after: "The order arrived late; however, the client accepted it." A colon introduces a list or explanation after a complete sentence.

Commas set off nonessential information. "Our Denver office, which opened in 2019, has 30 staff" means the company has one Denver office. "The office that opened in 2019 has 30 staff" identifies which office, so it has no commas. Business style commonly uses "that" for essential clauses and "which" with commas for nonessential ones.

Apostrophes show possession or contraction, never a simple plural. One employee's badge; two employees' badges; the children's room. Hyphenate a compound modifier before a noun ("a well-known brand") but not after it ("the brand is well known").

## Clear sentences

A dangling modifier has nothing to modify: "Walking into the lobby, the posters were bright" suggests the posters walked. Fix it by naming the actor: "Walking into the lobby, I noticed the bright posters." Keep items in a list parallel in form: "The role requires scheduling, budgeting, and training staff," not "scheduling, budgets, and to train staff."

Prefer active voice, where the subject acts: "The manager approved the request." Passive voice ("The request was approved") hides the actor; use it only when the actor is unknown or unimportant, or to soften bad news. Cut wordy phrases: "due to the fact that" becomes "because," "at this point in time" becomes "now," and "in order to" becomes "to."

Many business style guides spell out numbers one through nine or ten and use figures for larger numbers. Use figures for money, percentages, dates, and measurements. Follow the style guide your organization or event specifies, and be consistent.

## Edit and revise for the audience

Edit in passes: first content and organization, then sentences and word choice, then grammar and punctuation, then formatting and names. Reading aloud catches missing words. Proofreaders' marks include a caret for an insertion, three short lines under a letter to capitalize, a slash through a letter for lowercase, a looped line to delete, and a pound sign for adding a space.

Revision for audience changes vocabulary, detail, and tone. A message to engineers can use technical terms that a message to customers should explain or replace. Tone should be courteous and confident, focus on the reader ("you can now track orders"), and use positive wording where honest ("we can ship on Tuesday" rather than "we cannot ship until Tuesday").

> **Example**
>
> Revise for a customer audience: "Due to the fact that the SKU was mis-keyed into the WMS, your order was not able to be shipped by us until such time as the error was caught."
>
> "We entered your item number incorrectly in our warehouse system, so your order shipped two days late. We have corrected the error and refunded your shipping charge." The revision uses active voice, plain words, takes responsibility, and tells the customer what was done.

## Key terms

**Comma splice.** Two independent clauses joined only by a comma.

**Nonessential clause.** Added information that can be removed without changing which thing is meant; set off with commas.

**Dangling modifier.** A phrase that does not clearly modify any word in the sentence.

**Parallel structure.** Using the same grammatical form for items in a series.

**Active voice.** A sentence in which the subject performs the action.
