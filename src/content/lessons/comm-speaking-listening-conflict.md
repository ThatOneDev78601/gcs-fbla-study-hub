## Telephone and voicemail

Answer promptly, usually within three rings, with a greeting, the organization's name, and your name. Ask permission before placing a caller on hold and check back if the wait is long. When transferring, tell the caller who you are connecting them to and why, and give the colleague the caller's name and need so the caller does not repeat everything.

A complete phone message records the caller's name and organization, date and time, callback number, the message, and the action requested. Read the number back to confirm it. A voicemail you leave should give your name and number at the start and again at the end, state the purpose briefly, and say what you need and by when.

## Meetings and directions

A productive meeting has a purpose, an agenda sent in advance with time limits, the right people, and assigned roles: facilitator, timekeeper, and note-taker. The facilitator invites quiet members, keeps discussion on topic, and summarizes decisions. Minutes record decisions and action items, each with an owner and a due date.

As a participant, prepare, arrive on time, speak to the agenda item, build on others' ideas, and disagree with the idea rather than the person. When giving work directions, state the goal, break the task into ordered steps, show an example, give the deadline, and ask the listener to explain the task back. Following written directions means reading the whole instruction once before starting, then working step by step and checking each one.

Reading to understand a policy means finding who it applies to, what it requires, the exceptions, and the consequences. Judge information sources by currency, relevance, authority, accuracy, and purpose. An official policy page outranks a coworker's summary.

1. Before: send the agenda, goals, and any reading at least a day ahead.
2. Open: state the purpose and the decisions needed.
3. Discuss: take one item at a time; use round-robin for quick input.
4. Decide: confirm the decision aloud so everyone hears the same thing.
5. Close: review action items, owners, and dates; send minutes within a day.

## Active listening and nonverbal cues

Active listening means giving full attention, withholding judgment, asking clarifying questions, paraphrasing ("So the main concern is the delivery date, not the price?"), and summarizing at the end. Barriers include distractions, planning your reply while the other person talks, assumptions, and emotional reactions to certain words. Take brief notes of key points and check them with the speaker.

Nonverbal communication includes facial expressions, gestures, posture, eye contact, personal space, and paralanguage, which is tone, pace, and volume. When words and nonverbal cues conflict, listeners usually trust the nonverbal message. Meanings vary by culture: steady eye contact signals honesty in some cultures and disrespect toward elders in others. Ask rather than assume.

## Persuade, negotiate, and build consensus

Persuasion combines ethos (credibility), logos (evidence and reasoning), and pathos (connection to the audience's values and feelings). In negotiation, separate positions, which are what people say they want, from interests, which are why they want it. Two departments both demanding the same conference room may have different interests: one needs a projector, the other needs privacy.

Know your BATNA, the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. It tells you when to walk away. Aim for options that meet both sides' interests. Consensus means everyone can support the decision, even if it was not each person's first choice. It takes longer than a vote but builds commitment.

## Assertiveness, personalities, and conflict

Passive communication hides your needs; aggressive communication ignores others' needs; assertive communication states your needs while respecting others. An I-statement describes the behavior, its effect, and a request: "When reports arrive the morning of the meeting, I cannot review them. Could you send them the day before?"

People differ in style. Some prefer quick decisions and bottom-line summaries, others want detail and data, and others value relationships and harmony. Adapting your delivery to the listener is a skill, not a loss of honesty.

The Thomas-Kilmann model names five conflict styles: competing (assertive, uncooperative), accommodating (giving in), avoiding (postponing), compromising (each gives something), and collaborating (working together for a solution that meets both sides' interests). Each fits some situations. Collaboration suits important issues with time to talk; avoiding may suit trivial issues or a cooling-off period.

> **Example**
>
> Two coworkers argue during a team meeting because one keeps changing a shared spreadsheet's format, which breaks the other's formulas.
>
> The team lead pauses the argument and schedules a short private conversation. Each person explains the problem without blame. The lead paraphrases both interests: one needs readable formatting for a client, the other needs stable formulas. They collaborate on a solution: a locked data tab for formulas and a separate formatted view for clients. The lead confirms the agreement in writing and checks back in a week.

> **Try it yourself**
>
> A customer calls, upset that a promised callback never happened, and asks for a manager who is in a meeting. Write what you would say, using active listening, an honest statement of what you can do, and a clear next step. Then identify which conflict style you used.

## Key terms

**Paraphrasing.** Restating a speaker's message in your own words to confirm understanding.

**Paralanguage.** Vocal qualities such as tone, pace, and volume that add meaning to words.

**BATNA.** Best alternative to a negotiated agreement; the option if talks fail.

**Consensus.** A decision every member can support, even if it was not each one's first choice.

**I-statement.** A statement that describes a behavior's effect on the speaker and makes a request without blame.
