## Read the brief as a checklist

A design brief states the client, purpose, audience, required pieces, sizes, content, constraints, and deadline. Competitive event briefs add rules about software, originality, file submission, and presentation. Before designing, turn the brief into a checklist with one line per requirement and a column for where your work satisfies it. Separate firm requirements from your own creative choices.

This lesson uses a fictional brief, not the competition topic: a city farmers market needs a logo, a 24 by 36 inch poster, and a social media post announcing a new Saturday evening market for families.

| Requirement | Source in brief | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logo usable in one color and full color | Deliverables section | To do |
| Poster 24 x 36 inches, print-ready | Deliverables section | To do |
| Social post announcing evening hours | Deliverables section | To do |
| Audience: families with young children | Audience section | Guides tone and imagery |
| Must include date, time, location, and website | Content section | Check on every piece |
| All images original or properly licensed | Rules | Track in source log |

## Research, sketch, and compare alternatives

Research the audience, the client's existing materials, and competitors so the design stands out for the right reasons. Build a mood board of colors, textures, and type that fit the tone. Then sketch quickly by hand: 15 to 20 small thumbnails explore more ideas than one polished digital draft.

Choose two or three directions and develop them as rough digital comps. Compare them against the checklist and the audience, not just personal taste. Record why each direction was kept or dropped. These notes become evidence of your design process when judges ask how you got to the final.

## Feedback, revisions, and permissions

Ask for feedback from people who resemble the audience, with specific questions: What do you notice first? What is this announcing? Would you know when to go? Record the feedback and the change you made, or why you did not change something. Save versions with clear names so you can show the progression.

Keep a source log listing every font, image, and icon with its creator, license, and link. Use your own photos, licensed stock, or public domain material, and check that each font's license allows commercial or competition use. Images found through a search engine are not free to use just because they are visible.

> **Example**
>
> Testers say the poster's illustrated vegetables look great, but three of five could not find the time of the event.
>
> Revision log entry: "Version 3 to 4: Moved time and date into a high-contrast band below the title and enlarged it from 60 to 110 point; reduced the vegetable illustration by 20% to make room. Retested with four viewers; all found the time within five seconds." The entry shows a problem, a decision, and evidence the change worked.

## Present the work

Organize the presentation around the brief and the rating sheet: the challenge, your audience research, the concept and why it fits, how each piece meets its requirements, the tools and technical settings used, and what you learned from revisions. Show the final pieces large and clear, with a few process images rather than every draft.

Explain decisions in design vocabulary: hierarchy, contrast, alignment, color harmony, typeface choice, and accessibility. Be ready to say why you chose vector or raster tools, the document settings, and how the design stays consistent across pieces.

> **Try it yourself**
>
> Write a 60-second explanation of one design decision from a project you have made (or the farmers market example), using at least three design terms and one piece of evidence from feedback or testing.

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

A strong answer names the decision (for example, choosing an analogous green and yellow palette), explains it with design terms (color harmony linked to fresh produce, high contrast for the date and time, a rounded sans serif for a friendly tone that suits families), and cites evidence ("four of five parent testers described it as friendly and found the time immediately"). It connects the choice to the audience and the brief.

## Key terms

**Design brief.** A document stating a design project's purpose, audience, requirements, and constraints.

**Thumbnail sketch.** A small, quick drawing used to explore layout ideas.

**Mood board.** A collage of colors, images, and type that sets a project's visual direction.

**Comp.** A rough version of a design used to compare directions before finishing.

**Source log.** A record of every outside asset used, with its creator and license.
