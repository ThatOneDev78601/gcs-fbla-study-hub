## Layout principles

Visual hierarchy tells the viewer what to see first, second, and third, using size, color, contrast, and position. Alignment places elements on shared edges or a grid so the design feels organized. Proximity groups related items. Repetition of colors, fonts, and shapes creates consistency. Contrast makes important elements stand out. Balance can be symmetrical (formal, stable) or asymmetrical (dynamic, but still visually weighted). White space, the empty area, gives elements room and improves readability.

A grid divides the page into columns and margins so elements line up. The rule of thirds places key subjects along lines dividing the frame into thirds, which often feels more natural than centering.

> **Example**
>
> Critique a fictional event poster: the title, date, location, four sponsor logos, and a photo are all similar sizes and centered, and there is a different font for each line.
>
> There is no hierarchy, so nothing leads the eye. Fix it: make the event name largest, the date and location second, and the sponsors small in a row at the bottom. Use one or two font families. Align text to a left edge on a grid and leave white space around the title. Let the photo fill the top half to draw attention.

## Typography

Serif typefaces have small strokes at the ends of letters and feel traditional; sans serif typefaces are clean and modern and read well on screens. Script and display faces suit headlines and logos but are hard to read in body text. Pair at most two families, often one for headings and one for body text.

Kerning adjusts space between two specific letters; tracking adjusts spacing across a word or line; leading is the vertical space between lines, usually about 120% to 150% of the type size for body text. Comfortable line length is roughly 45 to 75 characters. Avoid all capitals for long text, and do not stretch or squash letters.

## Color and accessibility

Color harmonies come from the color wheel. Complementary colors sit opposite each other (blue and orange) and create strong contrast. Analogous colors sit next to each other (blue, blue-green, green) and feel calm. Triadic schemes use three evenly spaced colors. Monochromatic schemes use tints and shades of one hue. Colors carry associations that vary by culture, so test them with the audience.

Screens mix light in RGB (red, green, blue); web colors are often written as hex codes such as #1A5E63. Printing mixes ink in CMYK (cyan, magenta, yellow, black). Spot colors, such as Pantone colors, are premixed inks for exact brand matching. A bright RGB color may look duller in CMYK, so convert and proof before printing.

Accessible design lets more people use the work. WCAG requires a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1 for normal text and 3 to 1 for large text (about 18 point, or 14 point bold). Do not rely on color alone to carry meaning; add labels or patterns. Give images alternative text on the web.

## Raster, vector, resolution, and export

Raster images are made of pixels and suit photographs; enlarging them too much makes them blurry. Vector graphics are made of mathematical paths and scale to any size without losing quality, so logos and icons should be vectors. Raster editors include Photoshop and GIMP; vector editors include Illustrator and Inkscape.

Print resolution is usually 300 pixels per inch. An 8.5 by 11 inch flyer needs 2,550 by 3,300 pixels. Screens need fewer pixels per inch but specific pixel dimensions, such as 1080 by 1080 for a square social post. Print documents need bleed, extra image area (often 0.125 inch) beyond the trim line, and a safe zone inside it for text.

Illustrations can start with traditional drawing media such as pencil, ink, marker, or watercolor, then be scanned and refined digitally, or be drawn digitally from the start.

| Format | Type | Best use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| JPG | Raster, lossy compression | Photographs on the web |
| PNG | Raster, lossless, supports transparency | Screenshots, graphics with transparent backgrounds |
| SVG | Vector | Logos and icons on the web |
| PDF (print-ready) | Can hold both | Sending files to a printer, with fonts embedded and bleed included |
| AI, PSD | Native working files | Editing; keep for revisions, but do not send to most viewers |

## Key terms

**Visual hierarchy.** The arrangement that shows viewers what to look at first, second, and third.

**Leading.** The vertical space between lines of text.

**Complementary colors.** Colors opposite each other on the color wheel.

**Vector graphic.** An image made of mathematical paths that scales without losing quality.

**Bleed.** Extra image area beyond the trim edge so color runs to the edge after cutting.
