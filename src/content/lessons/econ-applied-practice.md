## Resources, goods, and utility

The four factors of production are land (natural resources), labor (human effort), capital (tools, machines, and buildings used to make other goods), and entrepreneurship (organizing the other three and taking risk). Money is not capital in this sense; it buys capital. Goods are tangible. Services are intangible, are produced and consumed at the same time, and cannot be stored.

Utility is the usefulness that makes people willing to pay. Businesses add value by creating each type.

| Utility | Created by | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Form | Changing raw materials into a product | A bakery turns flour into bread |
| Place | Making a product available where customers are | A vending machine in a gym |
| Time | Making a product available when customers want it | A 24-hour pharmacy |
| Possession (ownership) | Making it easier to own or use the product | Financing or rental options |
| Information | Telling customers about the product | Nutrition labels and online reviews |

- Business activities are often grouped as extractive (farming, mining), production or manufacturing, marketing and merchandising (wholesale and retail), and services.
- Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative given up. It applies to every choice, including a business's use of time.

## Productivity, specialization, and diminishing returns

Productivity is output divided by input. If 12 workers produce 960 units in an 8-hour shift, labor productivity is 960 / (12 x 8) = 10 units per labor hour. If new equipment raises output to 1,152 units with the same workers, productivity rises to 12 units per hour, a 20% increase. Higher productivity lets a business pay more or charge less without losing profit.

Specialization means each worker or business concentrates on a narrow task. Division of labor splits production into steps. Adam Smith's pin factory example showed that workers each doing one step produce far more than workers each making whole pins. Specialization raises skill and speed but can make work repetitive and create dependence on others.

The law of diminishing returns says that adding more of one input while others stay fixed eventually adds less and less output. It applies in the short run, when something such as factory space cannot change.

| Workers | Total output | Marginal output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 10 | 10 |
| 2 | 24 | 14 |
| 3 | 34 | 10 |
| 4 | 40 | 6 |
| 5 | 42 | 2 |

- Marginal output rises with the second worker because two workers can share tasks. It falls after that because the fixed space and equipment become crowded.
- Organized labor is workers acting together through unions. Unions negotiate wages, benefits, and conditions through collective bargaining. The National Labor Relations Act of 1935 protects the right to organize. The Taft-Hartley Act of 1947 allowed states to pass right-to-work laws.

## Cost and profit

Fixed costs stay the same at any output level in the short run. Variable costs change with output. Total cost is fixed plus variable. Marginal cost is the added cost of producing one more unit. A business earns the most profit by producing until marginal revenue equals marginal cost. Under perfect competition, marginal revenue equals the price.

> **Example**
>
> A shop sells candles for $18 each. Fixed cost is $200. Total cost is $320 at 10 candles, $420 at 20, $540 at 30, $700 at 40, and $900 at 50. Which output earns the most profit?
>
> 1. Marginal cost per candle for each block of 10: (320 - 200) / 10 = 12; then 10; then 12; then 16; then 20.
> 2. Profit at each level: 10 candles 180 - 320 = -140; 20 candles 360 - 420 = -60; 30 candles 540 - 540 = 0; 40 candles 720 - 700 = 20; 50 candles 900 - 900 = 0.
> 3. From 40 to 50 candles, marginal cost ($20) is greater than the $18 price, so those candles lose money.
>
> Profit is highest at 40 candles. The last block made has a marginal cost of $16, below the price, and the next block would cost more than it brings in.

## Elasticity and economic indicators

Price elasticity of demand equals the percent change in quantity demanded divided by the percent change in price. If price rises from $10 to $11 (10%) and quantity falls from 1,000 to 850 (15%), elasticity is 1.5, so demand is elastic. Revenue falls from $10,000 to $9,350. When demand is inelastic, below 1, a price increase raises revenue. Necessities with few substitutes, such as insulin, tend to be inelastic.

Gross domestic product (GDP) is the market value of final goods and services produced in a country in a year: consumption + investment + government spending + net exports. Real GDP removes inflation; nominal GDP does not. The inflation rate is the percent change in the Consumer Price Index: a CPI rising from 300 to 309 means 3% inflation. The unemployment rate is the number of unemployed people looking for work divided by the labor force.

The business cycle moves through expansion, peak, contraction, and trough. A recession is a significant, widespread decline in activity. Leading indicators, such as building permits, stock prices, and new jobless claims, tend to change before the economy does. Coincident indicators, such as employment and industrial production, move with it. Lagging indicators, such as the length of unemployment, change afterward.

- Fiscal policy is government taxing and spending, set by Congress and the President. Monetary policy is the Federal Reserve's control of interest rates and the money supply.
- Higher interest rates raise borrowing costs, slow consumer spending on cars and homes, and reduce business investment.
- Government affects business through regulation, taxes, spending, and antitrust laws: the Sherman Act (1890), the Clayton Act (1914), and the Federal Trade Commission Act (1914).

> **Try it yourself**
>
> A coffee shop raises its latte price from $5.00 to $5.50, and daily sales fall from 400 to 380. (a) Calculate the price elasticity. (b) Is demand elastic or inelastic? (c) What happened to daily revenue?

## Key terms

**Factors of production.** Land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship.

**Form utility.** Value added by changing the form of materials into a product.

**Diminishing returns.** The decline in added output as more of one input is added to fixed inputs.

**Marginal cost.** The added cost of producing one more unit.

**Price elasticity of demand.** How much quantity demanded responds to a change in price.

**Leading indicator.** An economic measure that tends to change before the overall economy.
