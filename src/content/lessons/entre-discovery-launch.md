## Discover and screen opportunities

Opportunities come from problems people already pay to solve, changes in technology or regulation, shifts in demographics and habits, and gaps that competitors ignore. Generate many ideas before judging any. Tools include listing daily frustrations, studying trends in Census and industry data, and the SCAMPER prompts: substitute, combine, adapt, modify, put to another use, eliminate, and reverse.

Screen ideas with weighted criteria. Score each idea on market size, customer urgency, the founder's skills, startup cost, and competition, multiply each score by its weight, and compare totals. Global opportunities follow the same screening plus questions about trade rules, culture, and currency.

## Test with customer evidence and decide

Before spending heavily, test the riskiest assumption. Interview potential customers about past behavior, not hypothetical opinions: "When did your bike last break, and what did you do?" is better than "Would you use a repair van?" Run a small test such as a landing page with a sign-up form, a pre-sale, or a minimum viable product (MVP), the simplest version that delivers the core value.

Set a decision rule before testing. For example: proceed if at least 15 of 50 contacted cyclists book a paid repair within two weeks. Stop or change direction (pivot) if not. A decision to stop is a useful result when it saves money.

## Fund the venture

Match the funding source to the business. A venture that will grow slowly and steadily rarely fits venture capital, and a business with no cash flow yet rarely qualifies for a bank loan. Compare what each source asks in return.

| Source | What the founder gives up | Best fit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bootstrapping (personal savings, early revenue) | Personal risk; slower growth | Low-cost starts; keeps full ownership |
| Friends and family | Possible strain on relationships | Small amounts; put terms in writing |
| Bank loan | Repayment with interest; often collateral and a personal guarantee | Businesses with cash flow or assets |
| SBA-guaranteed loan or microloan | Repayment; paperwork | Small businesses that do not qualify for conventional loans |
| Grants and competitions | Time to apply; restrictions on use | Specific purposes; no repayment |
| Rewards crowdfunding | Obligation to deliver rewards | Consumer products with a clear story |
| Angel investors | Equity and some control | Early growth companies |
| Venture capital | Significant equity, board seats, pressure for fast growth | Businesses that can grow very large |

- List startup costs in two groups: one-time costs (equipment, licenses, deposits, initial inventory) and monthly operating costs. Add a reserve, often three to six months of operating costs, because revenue usually starts slower than planned.
- Hire an employee when the work is ongoing, central to the business, and needs your direction. Use a contractor or outside firm for specialized or occasional work such as legal filings, bookkeeping, or a logo. Compare the full cost: an employee also brings payroll taxes, insurance, and management time.

> **Example**
>
> An angel invests $200,000 for 20% of a startup. What are the valuations?
>
> Post-money valuation is 200,000 / 0.20 = $1,000,000. Pre-money valuation is 1,000,000 - 200,000 = $800,000. The founders' share falls from 100% to 80%.

## Legal, tax, and protection setup

Complete the legal setup before the first sale. The steps below are typical for a small Maryland business; confirm each requirement with the state and county, since rules depend on the business type and location.

1. Choose a legal structure and register the business name with the state. In Maryland, businesses register with the State Department of Assessments and Taxation.
2. Get an Employer Identification Number (EIN) from the IRS if you will have employees or form a partnership or corporation.
3. Obtain required local and state licenses and permits, and check zoning before signing a lease.
4. Register to collect sales tax if you sell taxable goods or services.
5. Buy appropriate insurance, such as general liability.
6. Protect intellectual property: search before choosing a name, register trademarks, and use confidentiality agreements.
7. Open a separate business bank account and choose accounting software.

## Design daily operations

Standard operating procedures (SOPs) are written steps for recurring work, so quality does not depend on who is working. Map how an order moves from the customer to delivery, assign an owner to each step, and choose simple systems for scheduling, payments, inventory, and customer records.

Measure productivity with a few numbers, such as jobs completed per day or average time per job. Make risk decisions with the same logic as any business: avoid, reduce, retain, or transfer each risk.

## Key terms

**Minimum viable product.** The simplest version of a product that delivers its core value and allows learning from real customers.

**Pivot.** A significant change in a business idea based on evidence.

**Bootstrapping.** Starting and growing a business with personal funds and revenue.

**Post-money valuation.** A company's value immediately after an investment.

**Standard operating procedure.** Written steps for performing recurring work consistently.
