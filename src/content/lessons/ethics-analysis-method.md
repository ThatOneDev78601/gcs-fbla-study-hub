## Know yourself first

Ethical judgment starts with self-awareness. Values are the beliefs a person treats as most important, such as honesty or loyalty. When two values conflict, the person faces a dilemma. Knowing your values helps you notice when a situation tests them.

Biases distort judgment. Confirmation bias is favoring information that supports what you already believe. Implicit bias is an unconscious association, such as linking a group with a trait. A stereotype is an overgeneralized belief about a group. Self-serving bias credits success to oneself and failure to circumstances. Reduce bias by seeking disconfirming evidence, asking people with different views, and using consistent criteria.

Feedback is a tool for self-awareness. Ask specific questions, listen without defending, and look for patterns across several people. Empathy, imagining how a decision feels to the people it affects, is both a personal skill and a check on bias.

## Ethical frameworks

Strong analyses apply more than one framework. When they agree, the choice is well supported. When they conflict, explain why one consideration outweighs the other in this case. Laws set a minimum; an action can be legal and still unethical.

| Framework | Central question | Weakness to acknowledge |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Utilitarian (consequences) | Which option produces the greatest good for the greatest number? | Can justify harming a minority |
| Rights and duties | Which option respects everyone's rights and fulfills obligations, such as honesty and promises? | Duties can conflict with each other |
| Justice and fairness | Which option treats people equally, or unequally only for good reasons? | People disagree about what is fair |
| Virtue | What would a person of good character do? | Can be vague in specific cases |
| Common good | Which option best serves the community as a whole? | Community interests can be hard to define |

## A repeatable analysis

Use the same steps for any case so nothing important is skipped. Separate facts from assumptions, and write down what you would want to know but cannot.

1. Facts: list what is known, and mark uncertain facts.
2. Ethical issue: state the conflict in one sentence, such as honesty to customers versus protecting jobs.
3. Stakeholders: list everyone affected, including customers, employees, owners, suppliers, the community, and regulators, and what each stands to gain or lose.
4. Duties: identify legal requirements, company policies, professional codes, and promises already made.
5. Alternatives: generate at least three realistic options, not just yes or no.
6. Evaluate: test each option against the frameworks and the short- and long-term consequences.
7. Decide and justify: choose, explain why it is better than the alternatives, and answer the strongest objection.
8. Implement and monitor: describe who acts, how it is communicated, and how you will know it worked.

## Worked case

This fictional case is for practice only. A fitness app company discovers that a software error exposed some users' workout locations to other users for three weeks. Fixing it takes a day. Marketing worries that announcing it will hurt a product launch next week.

> **Example**
>
> Apply the analysis to the fitness app case.
>
> 1. Facts: an error exposed location data for three weeks; it is now fixable; the number of affected users is not yet known.
> 2. Issue: transparency and user safety versus short-term sales.
> 3. Stakeholders: users (privacy and physical safety), employees, investors, regulators, and the launch partners.
> 4. Duties: data breach notification laws may require notice; the privacy policy promised protection.
> 5. Alternatives: (a) fix quietly and say nothing; (b) fix, notify affected users directly, and report as required; (c) delay notice until after the launch.
> 6. Evaluation: (a) and (c) violate the duty of honesty and may break notification laws; users could face real safety risks. Utilitarian reasoning also favors (b) once long-term trust and legal penalties are counted.
>
> Choose (b): fix the error immediately, notify affected users with clear steps they can take, report to regulators as the law requires, and review testing practices. The strongest objection is lost launch sales; the answer is that trust lost through a later discovery would cost far more, and users' safety outweighs one product launch.

## Present the reasoning in writing and aloud

In a written analysis, use headings that match the required elements and the rating criteria, so a judge can find each one quickly. State the recommendation early, show the reasoning, and cite facts from the case rather than inventing new ones. In a live explanation, open with the decision and the main reason, walk through stakeholders and alternatives briefly, address the strongest counterargument directly, and close with the action plan.

During questions, listen to the whole question, acknowledge a fair point, and explain why your conclusion still holds or how you would adjust it. Changing your mind when given a good reason is a sign of ethical maturity, not weakness. Teamwork matters in team events: divide the parts, rehearse transitions, and make sure every member can explain the full reasoning.

## Key terms

**Ethical dilemma.** A situation in which important values or duties conflict.

**Confirmation bias.** Favoring information that supports existing beliefs.

**Stakeholder.** Any person or group affected by a decision.

**Utilitarianism.** Judging actions by their consequences for the greatest number of people.

**Implicit bias.** An unconscious attitude or association that affects judgment.
