## Read the statement heading

The income statement reports revenue less expenses for a period. A balance sheet reports assets, liabilities, and equity at one date. The statement of equity explains owners’ changes; the cash-flow statement groups operating, investing, and financing cash movements. Profit differs from cash because accrual timing, borrowing, and noncash expenses matter.

> **Work through an example**
>
> Revenue is $8,000 and expenses are $6,000, but only $5,000 of revenue is collected.
>
> Accrual profit is $2,000. Cash collections are $5,000; they alone do not establish total cash flow.

## Manage purchases and sales

Accounts receivable is money customers owe; accounts payable is money owed to suppliers. Match an approved purchase order, receiving evidence, and invoice before payment. Returns reduce what customers owe. An allowance estimates doubtful receivables. Depreciation allocates cost over useful periods; it is not a cash fund or a measurement of current resale value.

Straight-line annual depreciation = (cost − estimated residual value) / useful life. Equipment costing $1,100 with $100 residual value and five-year life has $200 annual depreciation. Debit depreciation expense and credit accumulated depreciation, a contra-asset. Under an allowance approach, a specific write-off debits the allowance and credits receivables; it does not recognize the estimated loss twice. Read whether a question asks for gross receivables or net realizable value.

## Interpret ratios

Current ratio = current assets / current liabilities. Working capital = current assets − current liabilities. Gross profit = net sales − cost of goods sold; gross margin divides gross profit by net sales. Net margin uses net income. Compare similar businesses and periods. Annual-report notes explain accounting methods, commitments, and risks; one good ratio cannot guarantee timely collections.

> **Work through an example**
>
> Current assets are $18,000 and current liabilities $9,000.
>
> Current ratio is 2.0; working capital is $9,000. Both describe liquidity but neither guarantees cash will arrive before bills.

## Key terms to remember

**Receivable.** An amount owed by a customer.

**Gross profit.** Net sales less cost of goods sold.
