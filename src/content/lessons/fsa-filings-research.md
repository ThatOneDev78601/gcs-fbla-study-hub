## Find the right documents

Public companies file reports on the SEC's EDGAR system. Search by company name or ticker, then filter by form type. Confirm the company's fiscal year end on the cover page, since many retailers end their year in a month other than December. Use the filing for the fiscal year the event names, and record the filing date and URL for every number you use.

This lesson uses a fictional retailer, Harbor Wholesale, to teach the method. Students research the assigned company on their own.

| Form | Contents | Use it for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10-K | Annual report with audited statements | Main source for yearly analysis |
| 10-Q | Quarterly report, unaudited | Recent trends within the year |
| 8-K | Report of a major event | Leadership changes, acquisitions, earnings releases |
| DEF 14A (proxy statement) | Shareholder meeting matters | Executive pay and board structure |

## Read the 10-K in a useful order

The statement of shareholders' equity shows why equity changed: net income added, dividends subtracted, share repurchases subtracted, stock-based compensation added, and other comprehensive income. A company can grow net income while equity falls if it returns large amounts to shareholders.

1. Item 1, Business: what the company sells, how it earns money, its segments, and its competition.
2. Item 1A, Risk Factors: management's own list of what could harm results.
3. Item 7, MD&A: management's explanation of why results changed, often with comparable sales and other operating measures.
4. Item 8, Financial Statements and notes: the income statement, balance sheet, cash flow statement, and statement of shareholders' equity, plus the auditor's report.
5. The notes: accounting policies for revenue, inventory, leases, and segments. These explain how the numbers were measured.

## Analyze across periods and against peers

Calculate each ratio for at least three years so you see a trend. Put the calculations in a spreadsheet with a cell reference to each source number. Retailers are usually judged on gross margin, operating expenses as a percent of sales, inventory turnover, comparable sales growth, and return on invested capital. Compare the company with two or three competitors that sell similar goods to similar customers.

> **Example**
>
> Harbor Wholesale reports (in millions): Year 1 revenue 40,000, gross profit 5,200, SG&A 3,800, net income 1,000. Year 2 revenue 44,000, gross profit 5,500, SG&A 4,000, net income 1,150. Cost of sales in Year 2 is 38,500, and average inventory is 3,850.
>
> 1. Revenue growth: (44,000 - 40,000) / 40,000 = 10%.
> 2. Gross margin: Year 1 5,200 / 40,000 = 13.0%; Year 2 5,500 / 44,000 = 12.5%.
> 3. SG&A as percent of revenue: Year 1 9.5%; Year 2 9.1%.
> 4. Net margin: Year 1 2.5%; Year 2 2.6%.
> 5. Inventory turnover, Year 2: 38,500 / 3,850 = 10 times, or about 36.5 days.
>
> Harbor grew sales 10% while gross margin slipped half a point, likely from lower prices or higher product costs. It offset that by spreading operating costs over more sales, so net margin rose slightly. Next, check MD&A for the stated cause and compare peers' margins before drawing a conclusion.

## Recommend strategic actions

A strong recommendation connects one finding to one action and states the expected effect, the risk, and how it would be measured. Limit the analysis to two or three actions that the evidence supports. State limitations, such as the use of one fiscal year, differences in competitors' fiscal calendars, or figures that management chooses to report without audit.

> **Try it yourself**
>
> Using the Harbor figures, write one strategic recommendation in four parts: finding, action, expected effect, and risk.

## Key terms

**EDGAR.** The SEC's public database of company filings.

**MD&A.** Management's discussion and analysis: management's explanation of results in the 10-K.

**Fiscal year.** A company's 12-month accounting year, which may not match the calendar year.

**Comparable sales.** Sales growth from stores open long enough to compare with the prior period.

**Statement of shareholders' equity.** A statement explaining the changes in each equity account during the period.
