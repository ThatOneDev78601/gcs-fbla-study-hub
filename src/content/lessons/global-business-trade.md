## Trade and barriers

Trade reflects different resources, skills, opportunity costs, and customer preferences. Exports go to foreign buyers; imports come from foreign suppliers. Tariffs tax imports, quotas limit quantities, and embargoes restrict designated trade. Agreements may lower barriers without eliminating standards or customs procedures. Evaluate effects on producers, consumers, and trading partners rather than assuming any barrier benefits everyone.

## Label currency units

Exchange rates express one currency’s price in another. A stronger home currency generally makes foreign goods cheaper at a fixed foreign price and home exports more expensive to foreign buyers. Label the units before multiplying or dividing. Freight, insurance, duties, and delay also belong in landed cost.

> **Example**
>
> An invoice is €1,000. The rate changes from $1.10 to $1.20 per euro before payment.
>
> The dollar cost rises from $1,100 to $1,200. The euro strengthened against the dollar; the unchanged euro invoice hides a $100 exposure.

## Choose a market-entry method

Exporting sells from home; licensing grants intellectual-property rights; joint ventures share an undertaking; direct investment commits assets abroad. More control often requires more resources and exposure. Research law, labor practices, infrastructure, language, and culture without stereotyping individuals. Contracts should clarify governing law, payment, delivery responsibilities, and dispute resolution. Ethical concerns include bribery and working conditions. Domestic rules cannot simply be assumed to apply worldwide. Localize dates, units, support hours, and meaning as well as words.

Trade alliances can lower internal barriers and create common rules; compare a free-trade area with a customs union, which also shares an external tariff policy. Geography changes freight routes, climate exposure, and access to ports. Infrastructure and economic development affect purchasing power and delivery capacity. History and political relationships can shape trust and restrictions. Digital communication lowers coordination costs but introduces time-zone, access, security, and translation challenges. These factors help explain why copying a successful home-market model may fail abroad.

## Key terms

**Tariff.** A tax on imports.

**Joint venture.** A shared business undertaking.
