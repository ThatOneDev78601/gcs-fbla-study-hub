## Organizations, services, and roles

Healthcare facilities include hospitals (acute care, critical access hospitals in rural areas, teaching hospitals), ambulatory surgery centers, physician practices, urgent care centers, long-term care (nursing homes and assisted living), home health and hospice agencies, rehabilitation centers, and behavioral health providers. Ownership can be nonprofit, for-profit, or government, such as Veterans Affairs hospitals and county health departments. Accreditation by organizations such as The Joint Commission signals that a facility meets quality standards.

A hospital is typically governed by a board, led by a CEO, with executives such as a chief financial officer, chief operating officer, chief medical officer, and chief nursing officer, and department managers below them. Administrative roles include practice manager, health information manager, medical coder, billing specialist, compliance officer, and patient access representative.

## Budgets and marginal decisions

An operating budget plans revenue (mostly payments from insurers and patients) and expenses (staff, supplies, facilities, and equipment). A capital budget plans major purchases such as imaging machines. Payer mix, the share of patients covered by Medicare, Medicaid, private insurance, and self-pay, strongly affects revenue because payers reimburse differently. Cost-benefit analysis compares a project's costs with its benefits, including quality and access benefits that are harder to price.

> **Example**
>
> A clinic considers opening on Saturday mornings. It expects 20 extra visits at an average reimbursement of $110. Extra costs are $1,200 for staff and $300 for supplies per Saturday.
>
> 1. Marginal revenue: 20 x 110 = $2,200.
> 2. Marginal cost: 1,200 + 300 = $1,500.
> 3. Marginal profit: 2,200 - 1,500 = $700 per Saturday.
>
> Saturday hours add $700 a week if volume holds, plus access benefits for working patients. Test assumptions with a three-month pilot and track visits, no-shows, and staff fatigue.

## Staffing, training, and communication

Healthcare hiring requires verifying licenses and credentials, background checks, and health screenings. Orientation covers policies, safety, infection control, HIPAA, and emergency procedures, and clinical staff complete competency checks. Staffing plans balance patient volume, required skill mix, and safe workloads; burnout and turnover are major costs.

Clear communication prevents errors. SBAR (situation, background, assessment, recommendation) structures handoffs between staff. Ethical leadership includes patient-centered decisions, fair scheduling, speaking up about safety, and protecting staff who report concerns.

## Records, technology, and access

Electronic health records (EHRs) store patient histories, orders, results, and notes, and interoperability lets systems share data between providers. Patient portals let patients view results, message providers, and schedule. Telehealth includes live video visits, asynchronous messaging and image review, and remote patient monitoring of readings such as blood pressure. Analytics track measures such as readmission rates and wait times. AI tools can assist with documentation and image review but require validation, oversight, and attention to bias. Access must be role-based and logged, and staff should view only the records they need.

## Billing, payers, and compliance

Managed care plans control costs through networks and rules: HMOs require in-network care and referrals, PPOs allow out-of-network care at higher cost, EPOs cover only in-network care without referrals, and POS plans combine features. Payment models include fee-for-service, capitation (a fixed amount per patient per month), and value-based payments tied to quality and cost. Medicare is federal insurance mainly for people 65 and older; Medicaid is a joint federal-state program based on need, run by each state, with CHIP for children.

HIPAA's Privacy Rule protects identifiable health information and limits uses and disclosures to the minimum necessary; its Security Rule requires administrative, physical, and technical safeguards for electronic records; its Breach Notification Rule requires notifying affected individuals without unreasonable delay and within 60 days of discovery. Fraud laws include the False Claims Act (false billing to government programs), the Anti-Kickback Statute (paying for referrals), and the Stark Law (physician self-referral to entities they have financial ties with). Upcoding (billing a more expensive service than provided) and unbundling (billing parts of a bundled service separately) are fraudulent. OSHA's Bloodborne Pathogens Standard requires exposure control plans, protective equipment, and safe needle devices. Agencies include HHS, CMS, the CDC, the FDA, the HHS Office of Inspector General, and state health departments.

1. Scheduling and registration: collect demographics and insurance information.
2. Eligibility verification and prior authorization where the plan requires it.
3. Service and documentation by the provider.
4. Coding: ICD-10-CM codes for diagnoses and CPT or HCPCS codes for procedures and services.
5. Charge entry and claim submission: CMS-1500 for professional claims and UB-04 for institutional claims, usually electronic.
6. Adjudication by the payer and payment posting from the remittance advice.
7. Denial management: fix errors and appeal when appropriate.
8. Patient statements and collections for deductibles, copays, and coinsurance.

>
> A clinic bills $260 for a visit. The patient's plan allows $200, the deductible is already met, and coinsurance is 20%. (a) What is the contractual adjustment? (b) How much does the plan pay and how much does the patient owe? (c) A coder suggests billing the visit at a higher level than documented because the patient was difficult. What is this, and what should happen?

## Key terms

**Payer mix.** The share of patients covered by each type of payer.

**Revenue cycle.** The administrative steps from patient registration to final payment.

**Capitation.** A fixed payment per patient per period regardless of services used.

**Upcoding.** Billing for a more expensive service than was provided or documented.

**SBAR.** Situation, background, assessment, recommendation: a structured handoff format.

**Minimum necessary.** The HIPAA standard of using or disclosing only the health information needed.
