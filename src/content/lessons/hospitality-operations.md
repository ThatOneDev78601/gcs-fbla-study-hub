## The industry and its sectors

Hospitality includes lodging (hotels, resorts, short-term rentals), food and beverage (restaurants, catering, bars), travel and tourism (airlines, cruise lines, attractions), recreation, and meetings and events. Demand for leisure travel is sensitive to the economy: in recessions, travelers cut trips, trade down to cheaper options, and book closer to the travel date. Business travel follows corporate budgets.

Organizations include the American Hotel and Lodging Association, the National Restaurant Association, Meeting Professionals International, and the Events Industry Council. Careers range from front desk agent and server to revenue manager, sales manager, executive chef, event planner, and general manager. Information sources include industry benchmark data, guest reviews, booking data, and trade publications. Current trends include mobile check-in and digital keys, contactless payment, sustainability programs that reduce food and linen waste, and travelers combining business and leisure trips.

## Measure lodging and food performance

RevPAR combines occupancy and rate, so it shows whether a discount that filled rooms actually raised revenue. Revenue management adjusts prices by demand, raising rates for high-demand dates and offering packages or discounts for slow ones.

| Measure | Formula | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Occupancy rate | Rooms sold / rooms available | 90 / 120 = 75% |
| Average daily rate (ADR) | Room revenue / rooms sold | $13,500 / 90 = $150 |
| Revenue per available room (RevPAR) | Room revenue / rooms available, or occupancy x ADR | $13,500 / 120 = $112.50 |
| Food cost percent | Cost of food sold / food sales | $8,400 / $28,000 = 30% |

> **Example**
>
> A 120-room hotel averages 60 rooms sold at $160 on weeknights. A proposed $130 rate is expected to sell 80 rooms. Which earns more?
>
> Current RevPAR: 60 x 160 / 120 = $80. Proposed: 80 x 130 / 120 = $86.67. The lower rate raises room revenue from $9,600 to $10,400 a night. Check that variable costs for 20 more rooms, such as housekeeping and amenities, do not exceed the $800 gain.

## Guests, peak demand, guarantees, and recovery

Accurate guest orders begin with repeating the order back, noting allergies and special requests, and checking before delivery. Accessible service under the ADA includes accessible rooms and routes, service animals, and willingness to adapt. Peak demand, such as a Sunday checkout rush or a Friday dinner, is managed through reservations, staggered staff schedules, cross-training, express options, and setting honest wait times.

For catered events, the client gives a guarantee, the final attendance number, usually 48 to 72 hours before the event. The client pays for the guarantee or actual attendance, whichever is higher. Kitchens often prepare a small overset, such as 3% to 5% above the guarantee. Service recovery means fixing a guest's problem quickly and generously enough to restore goodwill: apologize, fix it, add a gesture when appropriate, and follow up.

## Cash and card controls

Cash controls include assigning each cashier a separate drawer, counting banks at the start and end of shifts, requiring manager approval for voids and comps, using blind drops (deposits counted later by someone else), and two-person counts. Card payments require authorization at the time of sale. Businesses that accept cards must follow the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS), which means never writing down or storing full card numbers and security codes, using secure terminals, and limiting access. Chip and contactless payments reduce fraud. A chargeback occurs when a customer disputes a charge, and the business must provide records such as a signed folio.

## Products, security, selling, and events

Hospitality products combine tangible parts (the room, the meal) and service. Location (place) heavily affects value. Guest motives include rest, celebration, business needs, adventure, and status. Security includes key card control, never announcing room numbers aloud, protecting guest data, and practiced emergency plans. Selling includes upselling a room upgrade when it fits the guest's purpose and suggestive selling in restaurants.

An event budget lists revenue (registrations, sponsorships) and expenses (venue, food, audiovisual, staffing, insurance) with a contingency of around 10%. Staffing alternatives include in-house staff, temporary agency staff, contracted vendors, and volunteers, each with different costs, training needs, and reliability. Measure satisfaction with short post-event surveys, repeat bookings, and online reviews.

>

## Key terms

**Occupancy rate.** The percentage of available rooms sold.

**ADR.** Average daily rate: room revenue divided by rooms sold.

**RevPAR.** Revenue per available room: room revenue divided by rooms available.

**Guarantee.** The final attendance number a client commits to pay for at a catered event.

**PCI DSS.** Security standards businesses must follow when accepting payment cards.
