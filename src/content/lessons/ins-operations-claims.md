## Manage risk before insuring it

Pure risk can only cause a loss, such as a fire. Speculative risk can cause a gain or a loss, such as starting a business. Insurance covers pure risk. A peril is a cause of loss, such as wind. A hazard increases the chance or size of a loss. A physical hazard is a condition such as icy steps. A moral hazard is dishonesty, such as faking a claim. A morale hazard is carelessness because one is insured.

The risk management process identifies risks, measures their frequency and severity, chooses a treatment, and monitors results. Enterprise risk management applies this process across all of an organization's risks, including strategic, financial, operational, and reputational risks.

|  | Low severity | High severity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High frequency | Reduce (prevent and control losses) | Avoid the activity |
| Low frequency | Retain (pay losses yourself) | Transfer (insurance or contracts) |

- Self-insurance is planned retention, often with a fund set aside for expected losses.
- A captive insurer is an insurance company owned by the businesses it insures.
- Reinsurance is insurance for insurers. Treaty reinsurance covers a whole class of policies automatically; facultative reinsurance covers one risk at a time.

## Legal principles and regulation

Insurance in the United States is regulated mainly by the states, an arrangement confirmed by the McCarran-Ferguson Act of 1945. Each state has an insurance department; Maryland's is the Maryland Insurance Administration. State regulators license insurers, agents, and adjusters, approve rates and policy forms, and monitor insurer solvency. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) writes model laws that states may adopt. Federal law affects areas such as health insurance and flood insurance.

| Principle | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Insurable interest | The insured must suffer a financial loss if the event happens |
| Indemnity | Insurance restores the insured to the prior financial position, without profit |
| Utmost good faith | Both parties must disclose material facts honestly |
| Subrogation | After paying a claim, the insurer can recover from the party who caused the loss |
| Proximate cause | The covered peril must be the direct cause of the loss |
| Aleatory contract | The amounts exchanged are unequal and depend on chance |
| Contract of adhesion | The insurer writes the policy, so unclear wording is read in favor of the insured |
| Unilateral contract | Only the insurer makes a legally enforceable promise |

## Underwriting and rate-making

Underwriters decide whether to accept a risk and at what price. They classify applicants into groups with similar expected losses. Adverse selection occurs when people with higher risk are more likely to buy coverage, which underwriting tries to control. Many states allow insurers to use credit-based insurance scores, built from credit report data, for auto and home pricing, while some states restrict them.

Actuaries use statistics to predict losses and set rates. The pure premium is expected losses divided by the number of exposure units. The loss ratio is losses divided by earned premiums. The expense ratio is underwriting expenses divided by written premiums. Their sum is the combined ratio. Below 100% means an underwriting profit; above 100% means the insurer depends on investment income.

Insurance technology now includes telematics devices that measure driving, aerial images for property inspections, and automated claims processing. Each tool raises privacy and fairness questions that regulators review.

> **Example**
>
> An insurer earns $10 million in premiums, pays $6.5 million in losses, and spends $3 million on underwriting expenses.
>
> Loss ratio: 6.5 / 10 = 65%. Expense ratio: 3 / 10 = 30%. Combined ratio: 95%, so the insurer made an underwriting profit of 5% before investment income.

## Work a claim from notice to settlement

Actual cash value (ACV) equals replacement cost minus depreciation. A 10-year-old roof with a 20-year life that would cost $12,000 to replace has an ACV of $6,000. A replacement cost policy pays the $12,000 minus the deductible. Injury claims include special damages, which are measurable costs like medical bills and lost wages, and general damages, such as pain and suffering. Most states use comparative negligence, which reduces recovery by the injured person's share of fault. Maryland is one of the few states that still uses contributory negligence, which can bar recovery if the injured person was even partly at fault.

Property policies often contain a coinsurance clause requiring the insured to carry coverage equal to a percent of the property's value. If coverage is too low, the payment is reduced in proportion. Hard fraud is deliberately staging a loss; soft fraud is exaggerating a real claim. Insurers use special investigation units and data analytics to flag patterns.

1. Notice of loss: the insured reports the loss promptly and takes reasonable steps to prevent further damage.
2. Coverage verification: the adjuster confirms the policy was active and the peril and property are covered, and checks exclusions.
3. Investigation: photos, reports, receipts, statements, and repair estimates document the loss.
4. Valuation: the adjuster applies the policy's valuation method, deductible, and limits.
5. Settlement: the insurer pays, repairs, or denies in writing with reasons.
6. Subrogation: if another party caused the loss, the insurer seeks recovery.

> **Try it yourself**
>
> A building is worth $500,000. The policy has an 80% coinsurance clause, a $300,000 limit, and a $1,000 deductible. A fire causes $100,000 of damage. How much does the insurer pay?

<summary>Check your answer</summary>

Required coverage: 80% x 500,000 = $400,000. The insured carried $300,000, which is 300 / 400 = 75% of the requirement. Payment: 0.75 x 100,000 - 1,000 = $74,000.

## Careers and organizations

Producers sell insurance. An agent represents the insurer; a broker represents the buyer. Both need a state license for each line they sell. Other careers include underwriter, claims adjuster, actuary, loss control specialist, and risk manager. Common designations are the CPCU (Chartered Property Casualty Underwriter) and ARM (Associate in Risk Management) from The Institutes, the CLU (Chartered Life Underwriter) and ChFC from The American College, and actuarial credentials from the Casualty Actuarial Society and the Society of Actuaries. Industry groups include the NAIC, the Insurance Information Institute, and RIMS for risk managers.

## Key terms

**Subrogation.** The insurer's right to recover a paid claim from the party who caused the loss.

**Adverse selection.** The tendency of higher-risk people to seek more insurance.

**Combined ratio.** Loss ratio plus expense ratio; below 100% shows an underwriting profit.

**Actual cash value.** Replacement cost minus depreciation.

**Captive insurer.** An insurance company owned by the organizations it insures.
