## Why countries trade

A country has an absolute advantage when it can produce more of a good with the same resources. It has a comparative advantage when its opportunity cost of producing a good is lower. Trade benefits both sides when each specializes in its comparative advantage, even if one country is better at producing everything.

Trade barriers include tariffs (taxes on imports), quotas (limits on quantity), embargoes (bans on trade with a country), subsidies to domestic producers, and nontariff barriers such as product standards and licensing rules. The balance of trade is exports minus imports. A trade deficit means a country imports more than it exports.

> **Example**
>
> Country A can make 10 laptops or 20 shirts per worker-day. Country B can make 2 laptops or 10 shirts.
>
> Country A has an absolute advantage in both. Its opportunity cost of one laptop is 2 shirts, while B's is 5 shirts. B's opportunity cost of one shirt is 0.2 laptops versus A's 0.5. A should specialize in laptops and B in shirts, then trade.

## Exchange rates in practice

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in another. If 1 euro equals 1.10 dollars, then 1 dollar equals 1 / 1.10 = 0.909 euro. A 500-euro item costs an American buyer 500 x 1.10 = $550. When the dollar strengthens to 1 euro = 1.00 dollar, the same item costs $500. A strong dollar makes imports cheaper for Americans and makes American exports more expensive abroad.

Exchange rates change with interest rates, inflation, trade flows, and political stability. A business that will pay or receive foreign currency later faces exchange-rate risk and may use a forward contract to lock in a rate.

> **Try it yourself**
>
> A U.S. company sells machine parts for 2,000,000 yen. When the order is signed, $1 = 150 yen. When payment arrives, $1 = 160 yen. How many dollars did the company expect, and how many did it receive?

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

Expected: 2,000,000 / 150 = $13,333. Received: 2,000,000 / 160 = $12,500. The yen weakened, so the company received $833 less. A forward contract could have locked in the original rate.

</details>

## Import and export procedures

Importers classify goods using the Harmonized Tariff Schedule (HTS), which sets the duty rate. Most duties are ad valorem, a percent of value: a $20,000 shipment with a 4% duty owes $800. U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) processes entries, collects duties, and enforces trade laws. Many importers hire a licensed customs broker.

Exporters must check whether a license is required. The Export Administration Regulations (EAR), run by the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security, cover commercial and dual-use items. The International Traffic in Arms Regulations (ITAR), run by the State Department, cover defense items. The Treasury Department's Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) enforces sanctions on countries and persons. Most exports need Electronic Export Information filed in the Automated Export System when a shipment's value is over $2,500 per commodity classification or a license is required.

| Document | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Commercial invoice | Describes the goods, value, buyer, and seller; used to assess duties |
| Packing list | Lists the contents of each package |
| Bill of lading (or air waybill) | Carrier's receipt and contract of carriage; an ocean bill can serve as title to the goods |
| Certificate of origin | States where goods were made, which can qualify them for lower duties under a trade agreement |
| Letter of credit | A bank's promise to pay the seller when documents meet stated terms |

- Incoterms set who pays for and bears risk during shipping. EXW (Ex Works): buyer handles almost everything from the seller's door. FOB (Free on Board): seller delivers onto the vessel. CIF (Cost, Insurance, and Freight): seller pays freight and insurance to the destination port. DDP (Delivered Duty Paid): seller handles everything, including import duties.

## Trade agreements and alliances

A free trade area removes tariffs among members. A customs union also sets a common tariff toward outsiders. A common market adds free movement of labor and capital. Geography, history, and development level shape trade: landlocked countries face higher shipping costs, former colonial ties often shape language and trade partners, and developing economies may compete on labor costs while building infrastructure.

| Agreement or group | Members | Main feature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| World Trade Organization (WTO) | More than 160 members | Founded in 1995 to replace GATT (1947); sets trade rules and settles disputes |
| USMCA | United States, Mexico, Canada | Replaced NAFTA in 2020; adds rules on autos, labor, and digital trade |
| European Union (EU) | 27 European countries | Single market with free movement of goods, services, capital, and people; many use the euro |
| ASEAN | Southeast Asian countries | Regional economic and political cooperation |
| Mercosur | South American countries including Brazil and Argentina | Customs union with a common external tariff |

## Bribery, labor, and culture

The Foreign Corrupt Practices Act of 1977 makes it illegal for U.S. companies and people to bribe foreign officials to win or keep business. It also requires public companies to keep accurate books and internal controls. It contains a narrow exception for small facilitating payments to speed routine government actions, such as processing paperwork. The United Kingdom's Bribery Act has no such exception. Many companies ban facilitating payments entirely.

Global labor issues include child labor, forced labor, unsafe conditions, and low wages in supply chains. The International Labour Organization sets core standards. U.S. law blocks imports made with forced labor, so importers audit suppliers.

Culture affects negotiation, management, and marketing. In low-context cultures such as the United States and Germany, people state meaning directly in words. In high-context cultures such as Japan and much of the Middle East, meaning depends on relationships, setting, and nonverbal cues. Hofstede's dimensions compare countries on power distance, individualism, uncertainty avoidance, achievement orientation, long-term orientation, and indulgence. Use them as starting points, since individuals vary.

- Business travel requires a valid passport, the right visa for business activity, and attention to State Department travel advisories.
- An ATA Carnet lets a business temporarily bring samples or equipment into many countries without paying duties.
- Protect company data on trips: carry minimal data, use a VPN, and keep devices with you.

## Key terms

**Comparative advantage.** The ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another producer.

**Tariff.** A tax on imported goods.

**Harmonized Tariff Schedule.** The U.S. classification system that sets import duty rates.

**Incoterms.** Standard trade terms that divide shipping costs and risk between buyer and seller.

**Foreign Corrupt Practices Act.** U.S. law banning bribery of foreign officials and requiring accurate corporate records.

**High-context culture.** A culture in which meaning relies heavily on relationships and nonverbal cues.
