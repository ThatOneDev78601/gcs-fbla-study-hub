## Story types and news judgment

News judgment decides which stories matter. Common news values are timeliness, proximity, impact (how many people are affected and how much), prominence of the people involved, conflict, novelty, and human interest. Story types include hard news (events, reported quickly and objectively), features (people, trends, and places, often with a narrative lead), profiles, editorials and opinion columns (clearly labeled), reviews, sports, and investigative stories that uncover what someone wants hidden.

Media include print newspapers and magazines, broadcast, online outlets, newsletters, and podcasts. Each platform changes length and form: a print story, a 60-second broadcast script, a social post, and an online story with links and embedded video can all come from one reporting file.

## Build a reporting file

Evaluate sources by how they know what they claim (firsthand or secondhand), their possible interests, and their record of accuracy. A press release is a source with a purpose, not independent confirmation. Check images with reverse image searches and check social posts with the original account.

1. Background research: read earlier coverage, documents, and data before interviewing.
2. Source list: people directly involved, experts, and people affected, with contact details and how each is connected to the story.
3. Interview plan: open questions ("How did the shortage affect your team?"), follow-ups, and questions that check facts you already have.
4. Interview notes: date, time, place, exact quotes marked as quotes, and paraphrase marked separately. Ask permission before recording; Maryland generally requires the consent of everyone in a private conversation.
5. Verification log: each key fact, how it was confirmed, and by which source. Confirm names, titles, spellings, and numbers. Use documents and a second independent source for important claims.
6. Open questions: facts still unconfirmed, which do not go in the story until confirmed.

## Law and ethics

Libel is a published false statement of fact that identifies a person and harms their reputation. Public officials and public figures must also prove actual malice, meaning the publisher knew the statement was false or recklessly disregarded the truth, under New York Times v. Sullivan (1964). Private persons usually need to show only negligence. Truth is a complete defense, and honest opinion based on disclosed facts is protected. Invasion of privacy includes intrusion, public disclosure of private facts, false light, and using someone's name or likeness for commercial purposes without consent.

For student media, Tinker v. Des Moines (1969) protected student expression that does not substantially disrupt school, and Hazelwood v. Kuhlmeier (1988) allowed schools to restrict school-sponsored publications for legitimate educational reasons. Maryland's 2016 New Voices law gives student journalists added protection in public schools. Copyright protects others' photos, video, and writing; fair use may allow limited use for news and commentary, judged by four factors: purpose, nature of the work, amount used, and market effect.

The Society of Professional Journalists Code of Ethics has four principles: seek truth and report it, minimize harm, act independently, and be accountable and transparent. Promise confidentiality only when necessary and with an editor's approval, because you must keep it. Separate news from opinion, correct errors openly, and avoid conflicts of interest such as covering a club you lead.

## Leads, structure, quotes, and style

A summary lead gives the most important who, what, when, where, why, and how in about 30 words or fewer. A delayed or anecdotal lead opens with a scene or person, then a nut graf explains why the story matters. The inverted pyramid puts the most important information first so editors can cut from the bottom. Features often use chronological or narrative structure.

A direct quote uses the speaker's exact words in quotation marks. A partial quote quotes a key phrase. A paraphrase restates in your words without quotation marks but still needs attribution. Use "said" for attribution in most cases; it is neutral. Attribute opinions and disputed facts, not common knowledge.

AP style, widely used in news writing, spells out numbers one through nine and uses figures for 10 and above, always uses figures for ages and percentages, abbreviates months with dates only for Jan., Feb., Aug., Sept., Oct., Nov., and Dec., and capitalizes formal titles only before a name. Captions identify people left to right and describe the photo in present tense. Headlines use present tense, active verbs, and no unnecessary articles.

> **Example**
>
> Facts: The school board voted 5 to 2 on Tuesday night to start high school classes at 8:30 a.m. instead of 7:25 a.m. next fall, citing research on teen sleep. Buses will cost an extra $1.2 million a year. Write a summary lead and headline.
>
> Lead: "County high schools will start classes an hour later next fall after the school board voted 5-2 Tuesday to move the first bell to 8:30 a.m., a change expected to add $1.2 million a year in bus costs." Headline: "Board moves high school start to 8:30 a.m." The lead has the who, what, when, and cost, and the headline uses a present-tense active verb.

## Key terms

**Inverted pyramid.** A news structure placing the most important information first.

**Nut graf.** A paragraph explaining why a feature story matters, usually after an anecdotal lead.

**Actual malice.** Knowing falsity or reckless disregard for the truth, required for libel of public figures.

**Attribution.** Identifying the source of information or a quotation.

**Verification log.** A record of how each key fact in a story was confirmed.
