## Sources and types of law

The U.S. Constitution is the highest law; a statute or rule that conflicts with it is invalid. Statutes are laws passed by Congress or state legislatures. Administrative regulations are rules issued by agencies under authority given by statutes. Case law, or common law, comes from court decisions. Courts follow precedent under the doctrine of stare decisis, so similar cases are decided in similar ways. Treaties and executive orders are also sources.

Criminal law deals with wrongs against society, prosecuted by the government, with punishment such as fines or prison. Civil law deals with disputes between private parties, usually seeking money damages. The same act can lead to both: an assault can bring a criminal charge and a civil suit. The burden of proof differs. Criminal cases require proof beyond a reasonable doubt; most civil cases require a preponderance of the evidence, meaning more likely than not.

Substantive law defines rights and duties, such as what makes a contract valid. Procedural law sets the steps for enforcing them, such as how to file a lawsuit.

## The court system

Federal courts hear cases involving federal law or the Constitution (federal question jurisdiction) and cases between citizens of different states when more than $75,000 is at stake (diversity jurisdiction). Federal district courts are trial courts. The 13 federal circuit courts of appeals review district court decisions. The U.S. Supreme Court hears a small number of appeals it chooses to accept.

State courts hear most cases, including contract, property, family, and most criminal cases. Maryland's trial courts are the District Court and the Circuit Courts. Appeals go to the Appellate Court of Maryland and then to the Supreme Court of Maryland. Those two courts were renamed in 2022; older materials call them the Court of Special Appeals and the Court of Appeals.

Trial courts decide facts and apply law, often with juries. Appellate courts review the record for legal errors and do not hear new witnesses.

1. The plaintiff files a complaint, and the defendant is served with a summons.
2. The defendant files an answer, which may include a counterclaim, or a motion to dismiss.
3. Discovery: the parties exchange evidence through depositions (sworn oral questioning), interrogatories (written questions), and requests for documents.
4. Pretrial motions and conferences may narrow the issues or lead to settlement. Most civil cases settle.
5. Trial: jury selection, opening statements, presentation of evidence, closing arguments, jury instructions, and verdict.
6. Judgment and possible appeal.

## Alternative dispute resolution and administrative law

Administrative agencies such as the EPA, OSHA, and the FTC are created by enabling statutes. They make rules, enforce them, and decide disputes. Under the Administrative Procedure Act, most federal rules follow notice-and-comment rulemaking: the agency publishes a proposed rule in the Federal Register, accepts public comments, and then publishes the final rule. Administrative law judges hear agency enforcement cases, and their decisions can be appealed to courts.

| Method | Who decides | Binding? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Negotiation | The parties themselves | Only if they sign an agreement |
| Mediation | The parties, helped by a neutral mediator who suggests solutions | No, unless the parties sign a settlement |
| Arbitration | A neutral arbitrator who hears evidence and issues an award | Usually yes; courts rarely overturn it |

## Torts and remedies

A tort is a civil wrong, other than a breach of contract, that causes harm. Intentional torts include battery (harmful contact), assault (threat of immediate harm), false imprisonment, defamation (false statements harming reputation; libel is written and slander is spoken), invasion of privacy, trespass, conversion (taking someone's personal property), and fraud. Business torts include interfering with another's contract and unfair competition.

Negligence is failing to use reasonable care. The plaintiff must prove four elements: a duty of care, a breach of that duty, causation (the breach actually and foreseeably caused the harm), and damages. Defenses include assumption of risk and the plaintiff's own negligence. Most states use comparative negligence, which reduces damages by the plaintiff's share of fault. Maryland uses contributory negligence, which can bar recovery entirely. Strict liability applies without proof of fault to abnormally dangerous activities and to defective products.

Remedies at law are money damages. Compensatory damages repay actual losses. Punitive damages punish especially bad conduct. Nominal damages, such as $1, recognize a wrong without real loss. Equitable remedies include an injunction (a court order to do or stop doing something), specific performance (ordering a party to perform a contract, usually for unique items like land), and rescission (undoing a contract).

> **Example**
>
> A grocery employee mops a floor and leaves no warning sign. A customer slips and breaks a wrist, missing two weeks of work.
>
> This is a negligence claim. The store owes customers a duty of reasonable care, leaving a wet floor unmarked is a breach, the fall was caused by the wet floor, and the customer has damages (medical bills and lost wages). The store would raise any carelessness by the customer; in Maryland, contributory negligence could bar recovery if the customer was partly at fault. This is civil law, and the store is liable for its employee's act within the scope of employment.

## Key terms

**Stare decisis.** The doctrine that courts follow precedent from earlier decisions.

**Preponderance of the evidence.** The civil standard of proof: more likely than not.

**Discovery.** The pretrial process of exchanging evidence between parties.

**Arbitration.** Dispute resolution in which a neutral party issues a usually binding decision.

**Negligence.** Failure to use reasonable care, causing harm to another.

**Injunction.** A court order requiring a party to do or stop doing something.
