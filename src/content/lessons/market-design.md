## Build a message hierarchy

An effective advertisement has a specific audience and action. A headline attracts the relevant reader, supporting copy explains a benefit with evidence, and a call to action states the next step. Distinguish a factual claim from an opinion and check whether the evidence actually supports it. Storytelling connects a recognizable problem with a credible change; it should not invent customer results or conceal important conditions.

## Use design to guide attention

Contrast, alignment, repetition, proximity, balance, and whitespace make relationships visible. Group related information and establish a clear reading order. Typography includes typeface, size, weight, line spacing, and line length; decorative type should not obscure essential information. Color harmony can unify a composition, but use labels and contrast rather than color alone. RGB is used for screens, while print workflows often use CMYK; preview output in its intended medium.

> **Example**
>
> A poster for a beginner repair workshop contains five equal-size headlines, pale yellow text, and no registration instruction.
>
> Choose one main benefit headline, add the date and location in a secondary tier, use readable contrast, and place a clear registration instruction last. Keep fine-print conditions readable.

## Adapt without losing the brand

A consistent voice expresses the same personality across email, social posts, signs, and collateral. Adapt length, dimensions, captions, and action to each platform rather than copying an entire poster into every format. Email needs a truthful subject, readable opening, and clear next step. Social content needs context even when seen without sound. Use properly licensed illustrations and images, accurate captions, alternative text where supported, and proofread all dates and contact details.

- Common mistake: confusing visual complexity with persuasive information.
- Common mistake: assuming a digital color will reproduce identically in print.
- Common mistake: shrinking text until everything fits instead of editing the message.

## Typography, color, and drawing media

Serif type has small finishing strokes; sans-serif type omits them. Display faces emphasize personality, while body faces prioritize sustained reading. Kerning adjusts specific letter pairs; tracking changes spacing across a selection; leading is line spacing. Avoid stretching type or using many competing styles. Complementary colors sit opposite on a color wheel; analogous colors sit near one another. Harmony supports mood, but luminance contrast determines much of text legibility.

Vector artwork uses scalable paths and is useful for logos and diagrams. Raster artwork uses pixels and suits photographs or painted textures; enlarging a small raster image can reveal pixelation. Pencil, ink, paint, photography, and digital illustration offer different textures and production needs. For print, check effective resolution, bleed, safe margins, and printer specifications. For digital output, check responsive cropping, file size, color contrast, and readable text at the actual display size.

## Key terms

**Hierarchy.** A visual order that shows what should be noticed first, next, and last.

**Call to action.** A clear instruction for the desired next step.

**Brand voice.** The consistent personality and style of an organization’s communication.
