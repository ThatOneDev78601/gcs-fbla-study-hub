## Marketing creates an exchange

Marketing identifies needs and builds an offering that customers value enough to exchange money, time, or attention for. Advertising is one part of promotion; marketing also includes research, products, channels, pricing, selling, and customer relationships. A good product with the wrong distribution can fail even when its advertising is strong. Retailing sells to the final consumer, while marketing operates throughout the supply chain.

## Choose a target and a position

A market is a group of potential customers with needs and the ability and willingness to buy. Segmentation divides it into useful groups: demographic, geographic, psychographic, or behavioral. Select a reachable target, then explain why its members should prefer your offer. Cultural influences, friends, income, perceived risk, and previous experience can change buying decisions. Business buyers also evaluate specifications, reliability, total cost, and approval procedures.

> **Work through an example**
>
> A bicycle shop can serve commuters or weekend mountain bikers. Its research finds nearby commuters dislike unpredictable repair times.
>
> Target local commuters and position the service around dependable same-day repairs. Set capacity and appointment policies before advertising that promise.

## Build a coherent plan

Start with a situation analysis: internal strengths and weaknesses plus external opportunities and threats. State an objective with a metric, baseline, target, and deadline. Align product, price, place, and promotion with that objective; assign responsibilities and a budget. A strategy is the broad approach, while a tactic is a specific action. Research analysts, salespeople, merchandisers, and brand managers contribute different skills to the plan. Compare outcomes with targets and revise the action when evidence changes.

- Common mistake: choosing everyone as the target makes messages and resource decisions vague.
- Common mistake: treating sales growth as proof that one advertisement caused it; seasonality and price changes may also matter.

## How retail models evolved

Retailing developed from local shops and markets into department stores, supermarkets, shopping centers, specialist chains, ecommerce, and integrated digital-and-physical experiences. These models coexist because customers value different combinations of convenience, assortment, advice, price, and trust. Self-service shifts some selection work to shoppers; specialty retail emphasizes expertise or a focused range.

Ecommerce takes orders online but still needs payment, inventory, service, and fulfillment. A marketplace connects buyers with multiple sellers; a direct-to-consumer brand sells without a traditional retailer. Subscription retail provides recurring deliveries under agreed terms. Omnichannel retail coordinates channels, such as online ordering with store pickup and consistent stock information; merely having both a website and shop does not guarantee integration.

## Key terms to remember

**Segmentation.** Dividing a market into groups with shared characteristics or needs.

**Positioning.** The distinct place an offering should occupy in a target customer’s mind.

**Marketing mix.** Coordinated decisions about product, price, place, and promotion.
