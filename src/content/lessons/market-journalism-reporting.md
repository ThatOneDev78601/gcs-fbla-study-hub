## Define the story and find sources

A reportable story connects a clear development with people affected by it. Ask what changed, why it matters, what evidence exists, and whose perspective is missing. Primary sources include original documents, direct observations, and people with firsthand knowledge; secondary sources interpret other material. Evaluate expertise, proximity to the facts, incentives, date, and corroboration. A polished website or large following does not establish credibility. Keep a source log so every factual claim can be traced.

## Interview with purpose

Arrange a suitable time, identify yourself and the topic, and clarify recording and attribution expectations before proceeding. Prepare open questions followed by precise factual checks. Listen actively, take organized notes, and ask follow-ups when an answer is vague. Do not infer honesty from body language alone. Confirm spelling, titles, dates, numbers, and the context of quotations. Separate direct quotation, paraphrase, and your own observation in the notes.

> **Example**
>
> A student says cafeteria waste doubled this month. One social post repeats the claim, and a custodian recalls two unusually busy days.
>
> Seek comparable waste records, measurement methods, and dates; interview relevant staff and students. If no reliable baseline exists, report what is known and avoid presenting the doubled figure as established fact.

## Check before publication

Build a claim checklist and verify each item against reliable evidence. Corroborate contested claims independently and give subjects a fair chance to respond. Avoid treating two posts quoting the same original source as independent confirmation. Preserve original notes and supporting files securely. Organize photos, captions, consent or access information, and source credits during reporting. Tell the editor what remains uncertain early enough to adjust the story or deadline.

- Common mistake: writing the desired conclusion first and gathering only supporting quotations.
- Common mistake: confusing a witness’s interpretation with direct observation.
- Common mistake: promising confidentiality casually without considering whether it can be protected.

## Key terms

**Corroboration.** Independent evidence that supports or checks a claim.

**Attribution.** Identifying the source of information or a statement.

**Primary source.** Original evidence or firsthand knowledge about the subject.
