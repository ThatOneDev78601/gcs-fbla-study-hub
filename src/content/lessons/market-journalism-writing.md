## Choose a structure for the purpose

The inverted pyramid begins with the most newsworthy verified information, followed by context and supporting detail. A chronological account follows time; a Q&A preserves an interview format; an hourglass shifts from a news summary into narrative. A feature may open with a scene but still needs a clear explanation of why the story matters. News reports, analysis, editorials, and opinion columns serve different purposes and should be labeled accordingly.

## Write precisely and attribute clearly

A strong lead answers the most important questions without cramming every fact into one sentence. Use concrete nouns, active verbs, accurate grammar, and language appropriate to the audience. Diction is word choice; syntax is sentence arrangement; tone is the attitude readers perceive. Attribute information close to the claim, distinguish allegations from established facts, and use quotations to add voice or insight rather than repeat simple facts. Headlines and captions must remain accurate when seen alone.

> **Example**
>
> A meeting approved a pilot tutoring program for 30 students, subject to funding. A draft headline says “School guarantees tutoring for everyone.”
>
> Revise to reflect the actual limited, conditional approval. Lead with what was approved, explain the funding condition, and attribute projections to the person or document making them.

## Edit through a dependable production chain

First check substance: accuracy, logic, fairness, missing context, and organization. Then copyedit grammar, style, names, numbers, and formatting; finally proofread the final rendered version. Choose text for detail and searchability, audio for voice, or video for observable action, while providing accessible captions or transcripts where appropriate. Coordinate supporting images and credits, deadlines, editor feedback, and final approval. A correction should be transparent when a published factual error is discovered.

- Common mistake: polishing punctuation while the central claim remains unsupported.
- Common mistake: writing a dramatic headline that goes beyond the story’s evidence.
- Common mistake: changing a direct quotation to improve what the speaker meant.

## Sentence-level accuracy

Check subject–verb agreement: “The list of activities is ready,” because list is the subject. Make pronoun references clear, avoid sentence fragments, and separate complete clauses correctly rather than joining them with a comma alone. Prefer precise verbs and consistent tense. “The committee approved the plan” identifies the actor more directly than “The plan was approved.” Choose tone and vocabulary for the audience without losing accuracy. Read a sentence aloud to find ambiguity, then verify that editing has not changed the source’s meaning.

## Key terms

**Lead.** The opening that presents the story’s most important information or entry point.

**Inverted pyramid.** A structure presenting the most important news first.

**Copyediting.** Checking language, consistency, style, and factual details before publication.
