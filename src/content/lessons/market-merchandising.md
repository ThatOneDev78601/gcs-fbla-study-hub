## Plan the assortment

Merchandising selects, buys, presents, and manages goods for the intended shopper. A buyer or merchandiser studies demand, quality, brand mix, price points, seasonality, supplier reliability, and available space. Assortment width is the range of categories; depth is the selection within each. Too little depth can frustrate target shoppers; too much can tie up cash and create slow-moving stock. Use sales and inventory databases to compare performance by category, location, size, and vendor.

## Organize the selling environment

Visual merchandising includes store layout, lighting, signage, fixtures, and product presentation. A display is a specific arrangement within that larger experience. A planogram shows intended product placement and shelf allocation. Cross-merchandising places complementary products together, such as notebooks and pens, to solve a customer need. A focal point, balanced arrangement, readable signs, and clear prices guide attention; reachable products and uncluttered routes make the arrangement usable.

> **Work through an example**
>
> A school store stocks many novelty pen colors but frequently runs out of basic black pens. Its display hides prices behind the merchandise.
>
> Use category and size-level sales data to deepen the black-pen assortment, reduce weak variants, and place a visible price sign near a notebook-and-pen grouping. Monitor stockouts as well as sales.

## Maintain and evaluate displays

Inspect displays for dust, empty positions, damage, outdated offers, incorrect prices, and unstable fixtures. Restock without creating hazards, rotate seasonal materials, and dismantle and store fixtures carefully for reuse. Compare sales, conversion, stock availability, and shopper movement before and after a change, while noting other influences. Retail careers include buying, merchandising, allocation, inventory analysis, store management, and ecommerce operations; digital retail still needs assortment and fulfillment decisions.

- Common mistake: displaying only what looks attractive while ignoring availability and customer demand.
- Common mistake: claiming a display caused higher sales without considering a simultaneous discount.
- Common mistake: allowing decorative fixtures to obstruct accessible routes or product labels.

## Key terms to remember

**Planogram.** A diagram specifying merchandise placement and shelf allocation.

**Cross-merchandising.** Presenting complementary items together.

**Assortment depth.** The number of variants available within a product category or line.
