## Start with the decision

A useful research question names the decision and the uncertainty: should a store add evening opening hours, and would enough customers use them? Secondary data already exists, such as sales records or government statistics. Primary data is gathered for the current question through interviews, surveys, observation, or experiments. Begin with relevant existing evidence, then collect new information where a gap remains. Check publication date, population, definitions, and collection method.

## Match method to question

Interviews uncover reasons but do not establish how common a belief is. Observation shows behavior but may not reveal motives. A survey can estimate patterns if its sample represents the intended population; a voluntary social-media poll may overrepresent enthusiastic followers. Ask one neutral question at a time. Separate satisfaction, purchase intention, and actual behavior. Use consistent measurement periods and document missing responses rather than quietly removing inconvenient answers.

> **Example**
>
> A store asks only lunchtime shoppers whether it should open later. Eighty percent say no.
>
> The sample excludes people unable to visit at lunchtime. Survey the target neighborhood at varied times and inspect evening online orders before concluding that later hours have no demand.

## Use information responsibly

Marketing-information management continuously collects and organizes data for decisions; one research project addresses a particular question. Useful measures include transactions, average order value, returns, conversion, repeat visits, and stock availability. Compare segments and time periods using the same definitions. Collect only necessary personal information, explain its purpose, restrict access, and avoid publishing identifiable responses. Report uncertainty and limitations alongside the finding.

- Common mistake: a large biased sample remains biased.
- Common mistake: confusing correlation with causation; an experiment needs a meaningful comparison.
- Practice: write a research question, select a method, and name the most likely source of bias.

## Key terms

**Primary data.** Information collected specifically for the current research question.

**Sampling bias.** Systematic distortion caused by who is included or excluded.

**Conversion rate.** Desired actions divided by eligible visits or opportunities.
