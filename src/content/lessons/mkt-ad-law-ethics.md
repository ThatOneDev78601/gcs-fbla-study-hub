## Truthful claims and substantiation

The Federal Trade Commission Act prohibits unfair or deceptive advertising. An ad is deceptive if it contains a representation or omission likely to mislead a reasonable consumer about something material, meaning something that affects the decision to buy. The overall impression counts, not just literal words, so fine print cannot fix a misleading headline.

Advertisers must have a reasonable basis, called substantiation, for objective claims before running them. Health and safety claims need competent and reliable scientific evidence. Puffery, which is vague opinion such as "the best coffee in town," is not treated as a factual claim. "Twice as fast as Brand X" is a factual claim that must be proven. A "Made in USA" claim without qualification means all or virtually all of the product was made in the United States.

- Bait and switch is advertising a product the seller does not intend to sell in order to push a pricier one.
- Comparative advertising that names a competitor is legal if it is truthful; competitors can sue over false comparisons under the Lanham Act.
- Industry self-regulation, such as the National Advertising Division of BBB National Programs, reviews disputed claims.

## Endorsements, email, texts, and children

Under the FTC's endorsement guides, an influencer or reviewer must clearly disclose any material connection to the brand, such as payment, free products, or a family relationship. The disclosure must be easy to notice and understand, such as "#ad" at the start of a caption or said aloud in a video. Fake reviews and paying for positive reviews are prohibited.

The CAN-SPAM Act requires commercial emails to have accurate header information and subject lines, identify the message as an ad, include a valid physical postal address, and offer a working way to opt out, honored within 10 business days. Marketing text messages and automated calls generally require the recipient's prior consent under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act. The Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) requires verifiable parental consent before collecting personal information online from children under 13.

## Ethics beyond the law

An ad can be legal and still unethical. Questions to ask: Does it rely on harmful stereotypes? Does it exploit fear, insecurity, or a vulnerable group such as children? Would the target audience feel misled if they knew how the claim was tested or how their data was used? Is the edited image presented as realistic?

Advertising technology raises new duties. Programmatic buying places ads automatically, so brands need controls to keep ads away from harmful content. Retargeting follows people who visited a website, which some find intrusive. Privacy laws in several states give consumers rights to know and limit how their data is used. AI-generated images or voices of real people require permission and should not mislead viewers.

> **Example**
>
> A fictional smoothie brand wants to post: "Doctors agree: our smoothie boosts immunity!" It surveyed three doctors who were paid consultants.
>
> This is deceptive. "Doctors agree" implies broad expert consensus, three paid consultants are not that, and the immunity claim is a health claim that needs scientific evidence. A lawful alternative: "Each bottle has 100% of your daily vitamin C," if lab testing supports it, with no implied disease benefit.

## Key terms

**Substantiation.** The evidence an advertiser must have before making an objective claim.

**Puffery.** Vague, subjective praise that reasonable consumers do not take as fact.

**Material connection.** A relationship between an endorser and brand, such as payment, that could affect the endorsement's credibility.

**Bait and switch.** Advertising an item the seller does not intend to sell to push a more expensive one.

**COPPA.** Federal law requiring parental consent before collecting personal data from children under 13 online.
