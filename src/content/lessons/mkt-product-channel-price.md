## Product mix, standards, and warranties

A product mix is everything a company sells. Its width is the number of product lines, its length is the total number of items, and its depth is the number of versions of each item, such as sizes and flavors. Consistency is how closely the lines relate. A snack company with chips, crackers, and nuts has a width of three; if its chips come in six flavors and two sizes, that item has a depth of 12.

Grades and standards tell buyers what to expect. USDA grades rate meat and eggs, and standards organizations such as ANSI and ISO set specifications for safety and compatibility. A warranty is a promise about the product. An express warranty is stated in words or ads. Implied warranties arise automatically: merchantability means the product works for its ordinary purpose, and fitness for a particular purpose applies when the seller knows the buyer's special need. Under the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, a written consumer warranty must be labeled full or limited. A guarantee is a broader promise, such as "satisfaction or your money back."

## Positioning, branding, and the life cycle

Positioning is the place a product holds in customers' minds compared with competitors. A perceptual map plots brands on two attributes that matter to customers, such as price and convenience, to find open space. Branding includes the brand name, mark or logo, and the promise customers associate with them. Brand equity is the added value a strong brand earns. Strategies include family branding (one name across products), individual branding, private labels sold by retailers, co-branding, and corporate branding of the company itself.

Bundling sells several products together for one price, such as a phone with a case. It raises the average sale but can hide the value of each item.

| Life-cycle stage | Sales and profit | Typical strategy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Introduction | Low sales; often losses | Build awareness; skimming or penetration pricing; limited distribution |
| Growth | Rapidly rising sales and profit | Add features and distribution; build brand preference |
| Maturity | Sales level off; competition peaks | Differentiate, find new uses or markets, defend share |
| Decline | Falling sales | Cut costs, harvest, reposition, or discontinue |

## Channels of distribution

A direct channel goes from producer to consumer, such as a brand's website. Indirect channels add intermediaries: producer to retailer to consumer, or producer to wholesaler to retailer to consumer. Agents and brokers bring buyers and sellers together without owning the goods. Intermediaries add value through assortment, storage, financing, and reaching customers the producer cannot reach efficiently.

Distribution intensity depends on the product. Intensive distribution places convenience goods such as gum everywhere. Selective distribution uses some outlets, as for appliances. Exclusive distribution uses one outlet per area, as for luxury cars. Channel conflict occurs when channel members compete, such as a manufacturer selling online below its retailers' prices. Omnichannel retailing connects stores, websites, and apps so customers can buy online and pick up in store. Technology such as electronic data interchange (EDI) and RFID tags tracks orders and inventory through the channel.

## Pricing methods and math

Cost-plus pricing adds a markup to cost. Competition-based pricing follows rivals. Value-based pricing sets price by what customers believe the product is worth. Penetration pricing starts low to win market share; skimming starts high and lowers over time. Psychological pricing uses prices such as $9.99. Discounts include cash discounts for early payment, quantity discounts, and seasonal discounts.

Markup can be stated on cost or on selling price. A shirt costs $40 and sells for $60. The $20 markup is 50% of cost but 33.3% of the selling price. To convert a markup on cost to a markup on retail, divide by 1 plus the cost percent: 0.50 / 1.50 = 33.3%.

Pricing law and ethics: price fixing among competitors violates the Sherman Act. Charging different business buyers different prices for the same goods can violate the Robinson-Patman Act when it harms competition. Deceptive pricing, such as a "sale" from a fake original price, violates FTC rules. Price gouging during emergencies is restricted in many states.

> **Example**
>
> A retailer pays $24 for a lamp and wants a 40% markup on the selling price. What is the price?
>
> If the markup is 40% of the selling price, cost is 60% of it. Price = 24 / 0.60 = $40. Check: 40 - 24 = 16, and 16 / 40 = 40%.

## Selling policies and product information

Selling policies set expectations: returns and exchanges, price matching, layaway or financing, delivery, and warranties. Clear, posted policies prevent disputes. Salespeople obtain product information from manufacturer training, specifications, testing the product, and customer feedback, and they translate features into benefits for each customer's need. Customer service after the sale, including follow-up, installation help, and fair handling of problems, turns one sale into repeat business.

## Key terms

**Product mix width.** The number of different product lines a company sells.

**Implied warranty of merchantability.** An automatic promise that a product works for its ordinary purpose.

**Positioning.** The place a product holds in customers' minds relative to competitors.

**Selective distribution.** Selling through a limited number of chosen outlets in an area.

**Skimming pricing.** Setting a high initial price and lowering it over time.

**Markup on retail.** Markup expressed as a percent of the selling price.
