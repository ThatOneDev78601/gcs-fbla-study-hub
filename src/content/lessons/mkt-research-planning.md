## Marketing information and data decisions

Marketing information management collects, analyzes, and shares data for decisions. Start with the decision, not the data: "Should we add delivery?" points to specific questions about demand, cost, and willingness to pay. Secondary data already exists, such as Census figures, industry reports, and company sales records; it is fast and cheap but may not fit the question exactly. Primary data is collected for this purpose through surveys, interviews, focus groups, observation, or experiments.

Qualitative research, such as interviews and focus groups, explores why people act as they do. Quantitative research, such as surveys and sales data, measures how many and how much. A/B tests are experiments that show two versions to similar groups and compare results.

Businesses monitor measures such as sales by product and region, market share, customer acquisition cost, conversion rate, customer satisfaction, repeat purchase rate, and customer lifetime value. Ethical research gets informed consent, protects personal data, keeps promises about anonymity, and reports results honestly, including unfavorable ones.

| Sampling method | How it works | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simple random | Every member of the population has an equal chance | Requires a complete list |
| Stratified | Random samples from each subgroup, such as grade level | Needs subgroup data |
| Convenience | Whoever is easy to reach | May not represent the population |
| Quota | Fill set numbers per group, chosen non-randomly | Selection bias within groups |

## Write questions that do not bias answers

A leading question pushes toward an answer: "Don't you agree our prices are fair?" A double-barreled question asks two things: "Is our staff fast and friendly?" Fix them: "How would you rate our prices?" and separate questions for speed and friendliness. Offer balanced answer scales, include "does not apply" when needed, and pilot the survey with a few people before launching.

> **Example**
>
> A school store surveys 40 students who came to the store at lunch and finds 90% want later hours. Is that conclusion sound?
>
> No. A convenience sample of current lunch customers likely overrepresents students who already use the store. A stratified random sample across grades, including students who never shop there, would better represent the student body.

## Customer behavior and market selection

The consumer decision process has five stages: need recognition, information search, evaluation of alternatives, purchase, and post-purchase evaluation. Influences include psychological factors (motivation, perception, attitudes), social factors (family, friends, reference groups), cultural factors, and the situation, such as time pressure. Maslow's hierarchy suggests people address basic needs before higher ones such as esteem.

Market segmentation divides buyers into groups by demographics (age, income), geography, psychographics (lifestyle, values), or behavior (usage rate, loyalty, benefits sought). Choose target segments that are measurable, large enough to be profitable, reachable, and likely to respond. Then position the product for them.

## Situation analysis and the marketing plan

A situation analysis studies where the business stands. SWOT lists internal strengths and weaknesses and external opportunities and threats. A PEST scan reviews political, economic, social, and technological factors. Competitor analysis compares products, prices, and positioning.

A marketing plan includes an executive summary, situation analysis, objectives (specific and measurable), target market, marketing mix strategies (product, price, place, promotion), budget, implementation schedule, and evaluation measures. Employee actions connect to results: well-trained, satisfied employees provide better service, which raises customer satisfaction and loyalty, which raises sales and profit. Marketing plans should include training and incentives for frontline staff.

> **Try it yourself**
>
> A fictional pet grooming shop has 400 customers who visit an average of 5 times a year, spending $45 a visit, with a 40% profit margin. Customers stay an average of 3 years. (a) Calculate customer lifetime value (profit). (b) If a promotion costs $30 per new customer acquired, is it worthwhile? (c) Name one primary and one secondary data source the shop could use to decide whether to add cat grooming.

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

(a) Yearly spending: 5 x 45 = $225. Profit per year: 225 x 40% = $90. Lifetime value: 90 x 3 = $270. (b) Yes, if the new customers behave like current ones, since $30 is far below $270, though new customers may stay less long. (c) Primary: a short survey or interviews with current customers who own cats. Secondary: local pet ownership data or industry reports on grooming demand.

## Key terms

**Primary data.** Data collected firsthand for a specific purpose.

**Secondary data.** Existing data collected for another purpose.

**Leading question.** A survey question worded to push respondents toward an answer.

**Market segmentation.** Dividing a market into groups with similar needs or traits.

**Customer lifetime value.** The total profit expected from a customer over the relationship.

**SWOT analysis.** A review of internal strengths and weaknesses and external opportunities and threats.
