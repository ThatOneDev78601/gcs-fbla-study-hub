## Give each slide a job

Begin with the audience’s decision or question, then outline the sequence before designing individual slides. A title should communicate the point, not merely label a topic. Choose a layout that gives evidence enough room to be read. Theme and master-layout tools keep fonts, colors, repeated elements, and spacing consistent. Avoid manually decorating each slide into a different visual system. Put necessary detail in speaker notes or a supporting handout when allowed; a dense slide is not a substitute for an explanation.

## Choose and label visual evidence

Use a bar chart to compare categories, a line chart for ordered time trends, and a scatterplot to explore a relationship between two numerical variables. Label units, periods, categories, and sources. A chart cannot establish causation simply because two values move together. Avoid distorted scales, decorative 3D, tiny legends, and colors that are the only way to identify a series. Crop images deliberately without stretching their proportions, and add alternative text where supported.

> **Work through an example**
>
> A slide shows monthly sales, but the axis omits units and the chart uses a truncated vertical scale to exaggerate a small increase.
>
> Label the series and period, choose an honest scale for the comparison, and state the actual change. Explain whether the change is large enough to matter to the business decision.

## Rehearse the complete output

Use transitions only when they help show a meaningful change. Excess animation takes time and can distract from evidence. Confirm slide order, readable contrast, embedded media playback, and that linked files will be available on the presentation device. Rehearse with speaker notes, a timer, and the actual permitted equipment. Export a backup if the event permits it and verify that the backup still communicates the essential argument. Check the official guide before assuming internet access, sound, external monitors, or a question period are available.

- Practice task: create five slides explaining a small sales dataset, with a central claim, two evidence slides, a recommendation, and sources.
- Common mistake: pasting a full spreadsheet into a slide and assuming the audience can read it.

## Key terms to remember

**Slide master.** A shared layout system governing repeated slide formatting.

**Speaker notes.** Supporting presenter information associated with a slide.

**Aspect ratio.** The proportional relationship between slide width and height.
