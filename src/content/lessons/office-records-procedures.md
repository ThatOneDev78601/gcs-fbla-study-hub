## The records life cycle and filing systems

A record is any information created or received in business and kept as evidence, on paper or digitally. The records life cycle is creation, distribution and use, storage, retrieval, retention, and disposition (destruction or permanent archiving). A retention schedule states how long each record type is kept, based on law and business need. A legal hold suspends destruction when records may be needed in a lawsuit or investigation.

Filing systems include alphabetic (by name), numeric (by assigned number, useful for confidentiality and large volumes), subject (by topic), geographic (by location), and chronological (by date). Many offices combine them, such as customer files by account number with an alphabetic index.

- Alphabetic indexing rules (based on ARMA International guidance): index a person's name last name first, then first name, then middle name or initial.
- File "nothing before something": Lee comes before Lee Ann.
- Move an initial "The" to the end of a business name: The Bright Idea Shop is indexed Bright Idea Shop The.
- Spell out symbols such as & as "and." Ignore punctuation.
- Names beginning with numbers file before names beginning with letters.

>
> Put these in alphabetic filing order: Mary Ann Chen; Chen & Sons Plumbing; The Bright Idea Shop; Lee Ann Brown; Brown Bear Toys; 21st Century Tutors.

## Digital files and safe handling

A consistent file naming convention makes files easy to find and sort. A good pattern is date (year-month-day), topic, and version: 2026-11-04_budget-draft_v2.xlsx. Year-month-day dates sort in order. Avoid vague names such as "final2" and avoid spaces and special characters that some systems reject. Store shared files in an agreed folder structure rather than personal desktops, and keep one current version.

Business information can be public, internal, confidential, or restricted. Share confidential files only with people who need them, use access permissions instead of email attachments where possible, lock screens when away, and shred paper with personal or financial information. Report a lost device or a misdirected email immediately. Accuracy matters too: check figures and names before sending, since a wrong record can cause real harm.

## Email, calendars, and application choice

Manage email with folders or labels, rules that sort routine messages, and a habit of acting on each message once: reply, delegate, schedule, file, or delete. Calendars should show meetings with a clear title, location or link, agenda, and the time zone when people are in different places. Check others' availability before sending invitations, and include buffer time between meetings.

Choose the application that fits the task. Word processing suits letters, reports, and documents with formatted text. Spreadsheets suit calculations, lists that need sorting and filtering, and charts. Presentation software suits visual support for a talk. Databases suit large sets of related records that many users query. Collaboration tools suit shared editing and team messaging. Integrated tasks combine them, such as a mail merge that pulls names from a spreadsheet into a letter.

> **Example**
>
> A manager asks an assistant to send personalized letters to 200 customers with each customer's name and last order total.
>
> Keep the customer list in a spreadsheet with columns for name, address, and order total. Write the letter once in a word processor and use mail merge to insert each field. Preview several merged letters, check that totals display as currency, then print or send. This avoids 200 hand-typed letters and the errors they would bring.

## Workplace protocols and decisions

The chain of command is the line of authority from employees to top management. Bring questions and problems to your direct supervisor first unless a policy names another route, such as reporting harassment or safety issues to HR. Read policies for who they apply to, what they require, exceptions, and how to get help.

Time management at work means listing tasks, setting priorities with your supervisor's goals in mind, estimating time, and telling people early when a deadline is at risk. Ethical habits include honest timekeeping, protecting confidential information, and using company equipment for business purposes. When solving a workplace problem, define it, gather facts, consider options and their effects on others, decide, act, and check the result.

- Professional development includes setting career goals, seeking feedback, learning new software, and keeping your resume current.

## Key terms

**Retention schedule.** A policy stating how long each type of record must be kept.

**Legal hold.** A suspension of record destruction because records may be needed in legal proceedings.

**Indexing.** Arranging the units of a name in the order used for filing.

**Chain of command.** The line of authority and reporting in an organization.

**Mail merge.** Combining a document with a data list to create personalized copies.
