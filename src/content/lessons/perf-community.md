## Begin with community evidence

A community-service project should address a real need identified through relevant research, partner input, observation, or existing data. Define who benefits and what change the chapter hopes to support. For an annual chapter report, connect activities to the program of work and chapter goals rather than assembling an unrelated scrapbook. Verify the current official activity period; if a guide contains an older date range, seek clarification and preserve the source rather than silently changing eligibility.

## Plan participation and keep records

Set goals, tasks, owners, timeline, resources, budget, and partner responsibilities. Track member participation consistently, including which members participated and what they contributed. Calculate participation percentage using the defined membership denominator, not the number invited to one activity. Keep evidence such as authorized logs, expenditure records, delivered quantities, partner confirmations, and dated publicity. Protect participant privacy and obtain appropriate permissions for images or testimonials.

> **Example**
>
> A fictional chapter has 40 members. Twenty-four unique members help prepare 120 learning kits across three sessions; some attend every session.
>
> Chapter participation is 24/40 = 60%, not the sum of all session attendance divided by 40. Record 120 kits as an output; claim improved learning only if suitable outcome evidence exists.

## Distinguish activity from impact

Inputs are resources; activities are what the chapter does; outputs are immediate delivered products; outcomes are resulting changes for beneficiaries. Use measures that fit the purpose and collection method. Compare goals with actual results, explain limitations, evaluate partner and participant feedback, and identify specific improvements. Publicity can document community awareness but is not itself proof that the need was solved. A strong reflection describes what changed and why the next version would be better.

- Common mistake: counting the same member multiple times as unique participation.
- Common mistake: equating volunteer hours with a proven long-term outcome.
- Common mistake: reporting only successes and omitting meaningful constraints or lessons.

## Key terms

**Output.** An immediate product or service delivered by an activity.

**Outcome.** A resulting change for the intended beneficiaries.

**Participation rate.** Unique participating members divided by the defined eligible membership.
