## Map cash flow and goals

A household plan begins with goals, income, essential and discretionary expenses, assets, and debts. Net worth is assets minus liabilities; cash flow compares money received and paid during a period. Separate recurring bills, variable spending, and irregular costs such as repairs or annual fees. A monthly surplus is not fully available for a new goal if predictable future costs have been omitted. Prioritize goals by urgency, time horizon, importance, and flexibility, and state assumptions clearly.

## Balance reserves, debt, and protection

An emergency reserve provides accessible money for unexpected expenses or income disruption. Its appropriate size depends on household stability and obligations. Paying expensive debt reduces interest costs, while maintaining some liquidity can prevent a new emergency from creating more debt. Compare interest rate, fees, minimum payments, and repayment timing. Insurance transfers specified risks for a premium; deductibles, limits, exclusions, and coverage needs determine the tradeoff. The cheapest premium alone does not show the best protection.

> **Work through an example**
>
> A fictional household has a $200 monthly surplus, no reserve, and credit-card debt charging substantial interest. It also expects a $360 annual insurance bill in six months.
>
> First recognize the $60 monthly provision for the known bill. Allocate the remaining $140 between a starter reserve and debt repayment using the household’s risk and repayment priorities, rather than calling all $200 free spending money.

## Match education and retirement choices to time horizon

Compare college options using total net cost after realistic grants, not only advertised tuition; include housing, supplies, transport, and borrowing costs. Distinguish grants, earnings, savings, and loans. Retirement saving benefits from time and compounding, but investments involve risk. Diversification spreads exposure; it does not eliminate losses. Short-term essential goals usually need more liquidity and less market uncertainty than distant flexible goals. Taxes, account rules, employer benefits, and loan terms affect outcomes, so check current official guidance. Review the plan when income, needs, or assumptions change.

- Common mistake: treating a loan as free financial aid.
- Common mistake: investing an emergency reserve in a volatile asset without considering access and loss risk.
- Common mistake: presenting projected returns as guaranteed.

## Key terms to remember

**Net worth.** Assets minus liabilities at a point in time.

**Liquidity.** The ability to obtain usable cash promptly without a substantial loss.

**Deductible.** The specified amount an insured person pays before certain covered insurance payments apply.
