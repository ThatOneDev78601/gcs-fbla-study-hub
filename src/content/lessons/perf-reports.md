## Outline around the assignment

Begin with the current report instructions and rating sheet, then create a section outline matching the required sequence. Define the purpose, intended reader, central question, and supporting evidence before drafting. A concise executive summary states the problem, method, findings, and proposed action; it should reflect the completed report. Use descriptive headings and transitions so the reader can follow how one conclusion leads to the next. Do not assume that every event shares the same page limit or required sections.

## Show the evidence behind each claim

A strong paragraph makes a claim, provides evidence, explains its meaning, and connects it to the purpose. Cite facts, borrowed ideas, figures, and quotations close to their use, and maintain a source list with publisher, title, date, and location. Tables should have clear labels, units, periods, and source notes. Distinguish actual outcomes from forecasts and donated value from cash. Include relevant methods and limitations so readers can assess the evidence rather than trusting a confident tone.

> **Example**
>
> A report says “The project was extremely successful” and includes photographs of volunteers but no outcome data.
>
> State the specific goal, count participation and delivered outputs, describe how those counts were collected, compare outcomes with the target, and explain what the photographs demonstrate. Avoid claiming benefits that were never measured.

## Edit and check the final report

Review content first: missing criteria, unsupported claims, contradictions, and unclear reasoning. Then edit sentence length, jargon, grammar, and consistency. Use whitespace, readable type, and concise tables to reduce effort; an attractive layout cannot replace evidence. Export to the required format and inspect every page for clipped text, broken tables, small captions, missing citations, and excess pages. Confirm file naming and submission instructions from the current stage guide.

- Common mistake: using a resource link as the only explanation of an important result.
- Common mistake: shrinking text to meet a limit instead of removing repetition.
- Common mistake: presenting plans and completed activities in the same tense.

## Key terms

**Executive summary.** A concise account of the report’s purpose, main findings, and recommended action.

**Source note.** A reference identifying where information in a figure or table originated.

**Limitation.** A condition that restricts what the evidence can support.
