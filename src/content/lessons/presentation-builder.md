## Purpose, audience, and evidence

Write one sentence stating what the audience should understand or do at the end. Business presentations usually lead to a recommendation, so research the problem, compare options, and choose one you can defend. Use credible evidence: research studies, government data, and named experts, with dates.

Topics about groups of people need extra care. For example, research on generations at work, such as Baby Boomers, Generation X, Millennials, and Generation Z, often finds that differences within a generation are larger than average differences between generations, and that many differences reflect life stage rather than birth year. Strong presentations cite such evidence, avoid treating any group as all alike, and recommend practices that work for individuals, such as offering several communication channels, two-way mentoring, and clear expectations. Weak presentations repeat stereotypes. Students build their own competition presentation; the example below uses a different topic.

## Outline to a recommendation

A reliable business outline is: opening (a hook and the question you will answer), background (the situation with key evidence), options (two or three, with criteria), recommendation (your choice and why), implementation (steps, people, timeline, cost), and closing (summary and call to action). Every slide or section should support the recommendation.

> **Example**
>
> Annotated outline for an unrelated practice topic: "How should a 40-person company onboard remote employees?"
>
> 1. Hook: "Half of our new hires this year never met a coworker in person during their first month." (Uses the company's own data to show the problem is real.)
> 2. Background: survey results on new-hire confusion and turnover, with source and date on the slide.
> 3. Options: (1) a self-paced online module, (2) a buddy program, (3) a one-week in-person start. Criteria: cost, speed to productivity, connection.
> 4. Recommendation: buddy program plus a short online module, because it scores best on connection at low cost. (Directly answers the question.)
> 5. Implementation: who trains buddies, a 30-60-90 day checklist, cost estimate, and how success is measured (90-day retention and a new-hire survey).
> 6. Close: "Approve a three-month pilot with the next six hires."
>
> Each annotation shows how the section earns points: evidence, comparison with criteria, a clear choice, a realistic plan, and a specific request.

## Design slides that support you

Slides support the speaker; they are not the script. Use one main idea per slide, a headline that states the point ("Buddy programs cut first-month questions in half"), large readable fonts (generally 24 point or larger for body text), high contrast, and consistent layout. Show numbers as labeled charts with units and sources. Use accurate business vocabulary and define terms the audience may not know. Cite sources on the slide or in a final references slide.

Avoid paragraphs of text, decorative animations, and low-resolution images. Add speaker notes for your own reference if the rules allow them.

## Rehearse, set up, and answer questions

Rehearse with a timer and the actual file on the type of equipment you will use. Bring the presentation in two formats (such as the native file and a PDF) and check the current rules on what equipment is provided and permitted. For teams, assign each section to a speaker, rehearse handoffs ("Now Jordan will walk you through the costs"), and make sure every member can answer questions on the whole presentation.

When answering questions, listen to the whole question, pause, answer directly in a sentence or two, then give one supporting reason. If you do not know, say so and explain how you would find out. Do not argue with a judge; acknowledge good points.

> **Try it yourself**
>
> Take a recommendation you could make to your school or chapter, such as a new club fundraising method. Write a six-part outline using the structure above, with at least one cited fact, two options and criteria, and a specific closing request.

## Key terms

**Recommendation.** The specific action a presentation asks the audience to approve or take.

**Criteria.** The standards used to compare options, such as cost or speed.

**Headline slide title.** A slide title that states the slide's main point as a sentence.

**Stereotype.** An oversimplified belief that all members of a group share a trait.
