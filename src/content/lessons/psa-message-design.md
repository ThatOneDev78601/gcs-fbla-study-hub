## Research the issue and choose one behavior

A public service announcement (PSA) aims to change knowledge, attitudes, or behavior for the public good, not to sell a product. Effective PSAs focus on one audience and one action. "Teens" and "be safer" are too broad; "high school drivers" and "put your phone in the glove box before starting the car" are specific.

Use credible sources: government agencies, peer-reviewed research, and established nonprofits. Record each fact with its source and date in a fact-check log, and use current statistics. A single strong, verified fact is more persuasive than many weak ones. This lesson's examples use distracted driving, which is not the competition topic, so students research and design their own PSA.

## Design the message

State a learning goal: after watching, the viewer should know or do one thing. Choose an appeal: emotional stories create connection; facts and demonstrations build understanding; humor can make a message memorable but must not undercut a serious subject. Fear appeals work only when paired with a clear, easy action the viewer can take.

The call to action names exactly what to do: "Turn on Do Not Disturb While Driving tonight." Close with it on screen and in audio.

## Script and storyboard to the time limit

Check the current event guide for the maximum length and any required elements. At about 2.5 words per second for natural narration, a 30-second PSA holds about 75 spoken words, with room for pauses and visuals. Storyboard every shot with its duration and add them up. Time the edit and trim until it fits with a small margin.

> **Example**
>
> Write a 30-second PSA script for high school drivers about phone use.
>
> 1. 0:00-0:05: Close-up of a phone buzzing on a car seat. Sound: notification chime. No narration.
> 2. 0:05-0:15: Driver glances down; cut to the road ahead going blurry. Narration: "Reading a text takes your eyes off the road for about five seconds."
> 3. 0:15-0:22: Wide shot of the same car parked; driver puts the phone in the glove box. Narration: "At 55 miles per hour, that's the length of a football field, driving blind."
> 4. 0:22-0:30: Text on screen with narration: "Put your phone away before you start the car." Logo and source line.
>
> About 40 spoken words fill 30 seconds with room for visuals. The statistic is attributed on screen to its source and verified in the fact-check log. Every shot is original footage with signed releases.

## Originality and the showcase

PSA events usually require original work. Read the guide's rules on previously published video clips, music, and images; some guides ban them entirely regardless of license. Use your own footage and either original music or music the rules allow, and keep releases for everyone on camera.

In a live showcase, explain the research findings and why you chose the audience and behavior, the script and visual decisions, the techniques, equipment, and software used, and your sources. Bring the fact-check log and production notes, and be ready to say what you would change with more time.

> **Try it yourself**
>
> Choose an unrelated public issue such as sun protection or bike helmet use. Write a one-sentence audience, a one-sentence learning goal, a specific call to action, and one verified fact with its source and date.

<details>
<summary>Check your answer</summary>

A strong answer names a specific audience (for example, middle school cyclists who ride to school), a single learning goal (they will know that a properly fitted helmet greatly reduces the risk of serious head injury), a concrete action ("Do the two-finger fit check before every ride"), and one statistic copied exactly from a government or medical source with the publication date. Vague audiences, multiple goals, or unsourced numbers do not meet the standard.

</details>

## Key terms

**Public service announcement.** A message created to inform the public or change behavior for the common good.

**Call to action.** The specific action the PSA asks viewers to take.

**Fact-check log.** A record of each fact used with its source and verification.

**Appeal.** The persuasive approach a message uses, such as emotion, logic, or humor.
