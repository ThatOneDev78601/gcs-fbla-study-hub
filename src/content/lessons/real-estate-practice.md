## Property, terms, and valuation

Real property is land and anything permanently attached to it; personal property is movable. A fixture is personal property attached so that it becomes part of the real property, such as a built-in dishwasher. Other key terms include an easement (a right to use someone else's land, such as a utility line), an encroachment (something that extends onto a neighbor's land), a lien (a claim against property for a debt), and equity (value minus what is owed). Property types include residential, commercial, industrial, agricultural, special-purpose, and vacant land. Zoning controls land use.

Appraisers use three approaches. The sales comparison approach adjusts recent sales of similar properties (comparables) for differences. The cost approach adds land value to the cost of replacing the building, minus depreciation; it suits new or unusual buildings. The income approach divides net operating income (NOI) by a capitalization rate; it suits rental properties.

> **Example**
>
> A 12-unit apartment building rents for $1,500 per unit per month. Vacancy is 5%, operating expenses are $82,000 a year, and similar buildings sell at a 7% cap rate. Estimate its value.
>
> 1. Potential gross income: 12 x 1,500 x 12 = $216,000.
> 2. Effective gross income: 216,000 x 95% = $205,200.
> 3. NOI: 205,200 - 82,000 = $123,200.
> 4. Value: 123,200 / 0.07 = $1,760,000.
>
> Estimated value is $1,760,000. Note that NOI excludes mortgage payments, and a higher cap rate would produce a lower value.

## Careers, licensing, and regulators

A real estate agent (salesperson) must work under a licensed broker. A broker has more education and experience, can operate independently, and supervises agents. A REALTOR is a member of the National Association of REALTORS who agrees to its Code of Ethics. Licensing generally requires prelicensing education, a state exam, a background check, and, for agents, a sponsoring broker, plus continuing education; in Maryland, the State Real Estate Commission licenses and disciplines licensees. Other careers include property manager, appraiser, home inspector, mortgage loan officer, title examiner, and developer, and many agents run their own businesses.

Federal agencies matter too: HUD enforces the Fair Housing Act, and the CFPB enforces mortgage disclosure rules, including the Loan Estimate given after application and the Closing Disclosure given at least three business days before closing. Rules change; for example, a 2024 legal settlement changed how buyer agent compensation is offered and led to required written agreements with buyers before touring homes. Check current rules and dates before relying on them.

## Fair housing, disclosures, and tenancy

The federal Fair Housing Act prohibits discrimination in housing based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, and disability. Maryland and many localities add protected classes; check current state and county law. Prohibited practices include steering (guiding buyers toward or away from neighborhoods based on protected traits), blockbusting, redlining, and discriminatory advertising. Advertisements should describe the property, not the kind of person wanted.

Sellers and landlords of housing built before 1978 must disclose known lead-based paint hazards and provide the EPA pamphlet. States require additional property condition disclosures. Leases may be fixed-term or month-to-month; landlord and tenant duties and security deposit rules come from state and local law.

Ways to hold title include ownership in severalty (one owner), tenancy in common (shares that pass by will), joint tenancy with right of survivorship (surviving owners inherit automatically), and tenancy by the entirety (a form for married couples in states that recognize it, including Maryland).

## From listing to closing

Deeds transfer ownership. A general warranty deed gives the most protection, promising clear title against all past claims. A special warranty deed covers only the seller's period of ownership. A quitclaim deed transfers whatever interest the grantor has, with no promises.

Most buyers borrow. A conventional loan with less than 20% down usually requires private mortgage insurance; FHA, VA, and USDA loans are government-backed options. Rates may be fixed or adjustable, and points are fees paid to lower the rate. In the secondary mortgage market, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac buy conforming loans from lenders, and Ginnie Mae guarantees securities backed by government loans, which gives lenders cash to make new loans. Investors compare rental property (cash flow and appreciation, with management work), REITs (liquid and diversified), and renovation projects (higher risk).

1. Listing: the seller signs a listing agreement; the agent prepares a comparative market analysis to suggest a price and markets the property.
2. Offer and acceptance: the buyer's purchase agreement states price, earnest money deposit, contingencies (financing, inspection, appraisal), and closing date.
3. Due diligence: home inspection, appraisal for the lender, and a title search; title insurance protects against past defects.
4. Financing: the lender underwrites and approves the loan.
5. Final walkthrough and closing (called settlement in Maryland): documents are signed, funds are disbursed, and the deed is recorded in public land records.

## Agency and ethics

An agent who represents a client owes fiduciary duties, often remembered as OLDCAR: obedience to lawful instructions, loyalty, disclosure of material facts, confidentiality, accounting for funds, and reasonable care. A seller's agent represents the seller; a buyer's agent represents the buyer. Dual agency, representing both sides, creates a conflict and requires informed written consent where it is allowed. Commercial representation often involves leases, investment analysis, and zoning issues that residential agents rarely handle.

Listing advertising must be truthful, comply with fair housing, and include required brokerage information. Ethical cases often involve disclosure, such as an agent who knows of a leaking roof, or confidentiality, such as a seller's agent revealing the seller's lowest acceptable price.

## Key terms

**Fixture.** An item attached to real property so that it becomes part of it.

**Capitalization rate.** Net operating income divided by property value; used to value income property.

**Steering.** Illegally guiding buyers toward or away from areas based on protected traits.

**General warranty deed.** A deed in which the seller guarantees clear title against all past claims.

**Secondary mortgage market.** The market where existing mortgages are bought and sold, providing lenders new funds.

**Fiduciary duty.** A legal duty to act in a client's best interest with loyalty and care.
