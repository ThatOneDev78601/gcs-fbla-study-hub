## Retail in context

Marketing covers every activity that moves products from producers to customers, including research, pricing, and promotion. Retailing is the part that sells goods and services to final consumers for personal use. A wholesaler sells to businesses; a retailer sells to you.

Retail has changed form many times: general stores in small towns, department stores in growing cities in the 1800s, mail-order catalogs that reached rural buyers, chain stores and self-service supermarkets in the early 1900s, suburban malls and discount stores after World War II, and online shopping and omnichannel retail today. Merchandising roles include sales associate, cashier, stock associate, visual merchandiser, buyer, department manager, store manager, and loss prevention specialist.

## Open, operate, and close a register

Many systems offer an X report, which shows totals so far without resetting them, and a Z report, which closes the day and resets the totals. Voids and no-sale drawer openings usually require a manager's code because they are common fraud points.

1. Opening: count the starting cash bank by denomination and confirm it matches the recorded amount before the first sale. Place bills face up in the same order.
2. Sale: scan or key each item, confirm the total aloud, accept payment, and keep the customer's bill on the drawer ledge until change is given.
3. Making change: count up from the total. For a $13.65 sale paid with $20: "13.65, 70, 75 (two nickels), 14 (a quarter), 15, 20." That is $6.35 in change.
4. Returns: follow the posted policy; check the receipt or look up the transaction, inspect the item, refund to the original payment method, and get required approvals.
5. Exchanges: process the return and the new sale so inventory records stay accurate.
6. Closing: run the end-of-day report, count the drawer, remove the starting bank, and prepare the deposit. Record any overage or shortage and report it.

## Receive, mark, and route merchandise

When a shipment arrives, count the cartons against the carrier's bill of lading and inspect for damage before signing. Note any shortage or damage on the delivery receipt, since signing a clean receipt can waive the right to claim later. Inside, check the contents against the packing slip and the purchase order. In a blind check, the checker counts items without seeing the expected quantities, which prevents simply copying numbers.

Marking puts the price and identifying information on goods, using tags, labels, or shelf labels tied to the barcode. Some vendors pre-mark items, called source marking. Routing sends items to the sales floor or stockroom. Stock rotation places older dated goods in front so they sell first (first in, first out). Store goods safely: heavy items low, aisles clear, and fragile items protected.

> **Example**
>
> The purchase order lists 48 phone cases. The packing slip lists 48. The checker counts 44, and one case is cracked.
>
> Record 44 received and 1 damaged on the receiving report, notify the buyer, and file a claim with the vendor for 4 missing and 1 damaged. Pay only for the 43 usable cases unless the vendor sends replacements. Do not mark the damaged case for sale.

## Count, track, and protect inventory

A perpetual inventory system updates records with every sale and receipt. A physical inventory is a full count, often once or twice a year; cycle counts check a few sections at a time throughout the year. Out-of-stock items should be reported promptly so they can be reordered, since every empty peg is a lost sale. Warehouses and stockrooms track each item's location so it can be found and replenished quickly.

Shrinkage is the loss of inventory from shoplifting, employee theft, damage, and paperwork errors. Good customer service is a strong deterrent: greeting every customer tells would-be thieves they are noticed. If you suspect shoplifting, follow store policy exactly. Typically that means staying visible, offering help, and notifying a manager or loss prevention. Never chase, touch, or accuse a customer, which risks injury and false-arrest claims.

## Build, maintain, and take down displays

Visual merchandising is the presentation of the whole store: layout, fixtures, lighting, signage, and how merchandise is arranged. A display is one specific arrangement of merchandise, such as a window or an endcap. Every display has merchandise, props, signs, and lighting, and should have one focal point.

Common arrangements are the pyramid (a triangle leading the eye to the top), step (items at rising levels), zigzag, repetition (identical items in a row), and radiation (items spreading from a center). Maintain displays daily: dust, refill from stock, straighten, and check that signs match current prices. To take a display down, remove the merchandise first and return it to stock, then remove props, clean the area, and store fixtures and props labeled so they can be reused.

## Key terms

**Retailing.** Selling goods and services to final consumers for personal use.

**Z report.** An end-of-day register report that totals and resets the day's transactions.

**Blind check.** Counting received goods without seeing the expected quantities.

**Stock rotation.** Moving older goods to the front so they sell first.

**Visual merchandising.** The presentation of the entire store to attract customers and encourage sales.
