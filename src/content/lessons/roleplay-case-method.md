## How role-play and case events work

Many FBLA test events add a live stage at state or national level where competitors receive an unfamiliar scenario, prepare for a set time, and present a solution to a judge who plays a role such as a manager or client. The judge scores the performance indicators listed in the case, along with communication and problem-solving. Formats, preparation time, permitted materials, and team sizes vary by event and stage, so read the current guidelines for your event.

The skills transfer across events. A Customer Service, Hospitality, Marketing, International Business, Entrepreneurship, or Sports and Entertainment case all reward the same habits: answer the actual question, use the event's knowledge, support recommendations with reasons, and communicate as a professional in the assigned role.

## The preparation routine

Divide preparation time roughly in thirds: understand, plan, and rehearse the opening and close in your head. Write notes as a short outline with the indicators as headings, if notes are allowed.

1. Read the whole case once quickly, then again slowly, underlining the task, your role, the judge's role, and every performance indicator.
2. Restate the problem in one sentence. Many weak presentations solve the wrong problem.
3. List facts from the case you can use, such as numbers, constraints, and the client's goals.
4. Choose a recommendation and two or three supporting actions, each tied to a performance indicator.
5. Do quick calculations if the case gives numbers, such as cost, break-even, or a percentage change.
6. Plan an opening that greets the judge in role, a body that covers each indicator in order, and a close that summarizes and asks for agreement or next steps.
7. Predict two questions the judge may ask and plan short answers.

## Worked case

Fictional practice case: You are the assistant manager of a 60-room hotel near a minor-league ballpark. Weekend occupancy is 95%, but weekday occupancy is 48%. The general manager (judge) wants a plan to raise weekday occupancy. Performance indicators: explain the nature of target marketing, describe pricing strategies, explain the role of promotion, and demonstrate customer service skills. Preparation time is 10 minutes.

> **Example**
>
> Plan the presentation for the hotel case.
>
> 1. Problem: weekday rooms are empty while weekends sell out.
> 2. Target marketing: identify weekday segments, such as business travelers, youth sports teams in midweek tournaments, and visiting team families. Explain that targeting focuses limited budget on groups most likely to book.
> 3. Pricing: offer a weekday corporate rate and group rates for teams; a 20% discount that raises occupancy from 48% to 60% can increase revenue if the room rate stays above variable cost per room.
> 4. Promotion: partner with the ballpark for a stay-and-game package, email local companies with a corporate rate card, and list team rates with youth sports organizers.
> 5. Customer service: add early breakfast and late checkout for business guests and train front-desk staff to ask about guests' plans and recommend services.
> 6. Close: summarize, propose a 90-day trial measured by weekday occupancy and revenue per available room, and ask for approval.
>
> Each part is labeled with the indicator it addresses, uses the case's numbers, and ends with a measurable next step. The presenter speaks to the general manager as a colleague, not as a student reciting definitions.

## Answer questions and self-review

When the judge asks a question, pause, answer directly first, then give the reason. If you do not know a fact, say how you would find out rather than inventing it. If the judge raises an objection, acknowledge it and adjust: "That's a fair concern about discounting. We could limit the rate to Sunday through Wednesday so it does not compete with weekends."

After each practice, score yourself against the indicators: Did you address each one by name or clearly? Did you use case facts? Was the recommendation specific? Did you finish on time? Record one thing to improve next time.

> **Try it yourself**
>
> Practice case: You manage customer service for a fictional internet provider. A small business owner (judge) is angry after a two-day outage cost them online orders. Indicators: demonstrate empathy, explain the company's service recovery options, handle a difficult customer, and explain the importance of follow-up. Plan your opening, three main points, and close in 10 minutes, then deliver it aloud in 5.

A strong plan opens by thanking the owner for meeting, apologizing sincerely, and paraphrasing the impact ("you lost two days of online orders"). Points: (1) empathy and listening, letting the owner explain without interruption; (2) recovery options within authority, such as a service credit for the outage days, a priority repair contact, and a backup connection offer, explained clearly, with escalation for any claim beyond policy; (3) prevention, explaining the cause if known and what changed. The close confirms the agreed remedy in writing and schedules a follow-up call in one week, explaining that follow-up rebuilds trust and catches remaining problems.

## Key terms

**Performance indicator.** A specific skill or knowledge item listed in a case that the judge scores.

**Role-play.** A live event format in which the competitor presents to a judge playing a business role.

**Recommendation.** The specific course of action the presenter proposes, supported by reasons.

**Self-review.** Scoring your own practice against the indicators to find what to improve.
