## Prepare: product and customer

Know the product completely: features, how they work, limits, price, warranty, and how it compares with alternatives, including competitors' strengths. List evidence for each claim, such as test results, reviews, or a live demonstration. Research the buyer's situation if it is given: industry, size, current solution, and likely concerns.

Translate features into benefits with the FAB method: the feature (what it is), the advantage (what it does), and the benefit (what that means for this buyer). "The tablet has a 12-hour battery (feature), so it lasts a full shift (advantage), which means your servers never lose an order mid-shift (benefit)."

## The selling sequence

Closing techniques include the trial close ("How does that fit your kitchen?"), the alternative choice ("Would the 10-unit or 15-unit package work better?"), the summary close (restating the benefits the buyer agreed with), and the direct request. Honest urgency, such as a real promotion end date, is fine; invented urgency is not.

1. Approach: greet the buyer, introduce yourself, and set a friendly, professional tone.
2. Discover needs: ask open questions before presenting. A useful sequence is situation ("How do you take orders now?"), problem ("Where do mistakes happen?"), implication ("What does a wrong order cost you?"), and need-payoff ("How would it help if orders went straight to the kitchen?").
3. Present and demonstrate: show only the features that meet the needs you heard, and let the buyer handle the product when possible.
4. Handle objections: listen fully, acknowledge, ask a question to understand, and respond with facts.
5. Close: ask for a decision or an appropriate next step.
6. Follow up: confirm details, delivery, and satisfaction.

## Handle interruptions and objections

In an interactive presentation, the buyer may interrupt at any time. Treat each question as a buying signal. Answer it directly, connect it to a need, and return to your plan without losing your place. Prepare by listing the ten most likely questions and objections and rehearsing short answers.

Common objections concern price, need, timing, the product, or the company. The feel-felt-found response acknowledges the feeling ("I understand how you feel"), shows others shared it ("other owners felt the same"), and shares what they found ("they found the order accuracy paid for the tablets in four months"). Use real evidence, not invented testimonials. If the objection is valid, admit it and show how the overall value still fits, or recommend a better alternative.

> **Example**
>
> Fictional product: a restaurant ordering tablet. The buyer interrupts: "This costs $3,000 more than our paper tickets."
>
> Acknowledge and explore: "That is a real cost. Can I ask how many orders a week need to be remade?" Buyer: "Maybe 25, about $15 each." Respond with their numbers: "That is about $375 a week, or $19,500 a year. If the tablets cut remakes by even half, they would pay for themselves in about four months." Then close a step: "Would a two-week trial in one section help you see the difference?"

## Setup, team roles, and backup

Plan the physical setup: where the product sits, what the buyer can touch, and how visuals are displayed. Check technology limits in the event rules, such as whether internet or electrical outlets are provided, and have a backup such as printed visuals or an offline video. For teams, assign roles (opener, demonstrator, objection handler, closer), plan smooth handoffs, and make sure every member can answer any question.

Review the rating sheet and rehearse a full pitch for a product unrelated to your competition product. Time each section and practice with a partner who interrupts unpredictably.

> **Try it yourself**
>
> Fictional product: a $40 smart water bottle that reminds the owner to drink and tracks intake in an app. Buyer: a gym manager considering selling it at the front desk. Write two discovery questions, one FAB statement, a response to "Our members won't pay $40 for a water bottle," and a close.

## Key terms

**Consultative selling.** Selling by understanding the buyer's needs and recommending solutions that fit.

**FAB.** Feature, advantage, benefit: a method for turning product facts into buyer value.

**Trial close.** A question that checks the buyer's readiness without asking for the final decision.

**Objection.** A buyer's concern or reason for hesitating.

**Buying signal.** A comment or question showing the buyer is considering a purchase.
