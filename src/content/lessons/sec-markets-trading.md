## How securities are issued

A company raises money in the primary market. In an initial public offering (IPO), an investment bank underwrites the shares. In a firm commitment, the underwriter buys the entire issue and resells it, taking the risk. In a best efforts deal, the underwriter only sells what it can. The company files a registration statement with the SEC, and investors receive a prospectus describing the business, risks, and use of the money.

After issue, shares trade in the secondary market on exchanges such as the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq, or over the counter. Market makers quote a bid, the price they will pay, and an ask, the price at which they will sell. The difference is the spread.

| Law or body | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Securities Act of 1933 | Registration and disclosure for new issues |
| Securities Exchange Act of 1934 | Created the SEC; regulates secondary trading, exchanges, and brokers |
| Investment Company Act of 1940 | Regulates mutual funds and other investment companies |
| Investment Advisers Act of 1940 | Regulates people paid to give investment advice |
| FINRA | Self-regulatory organization that licenses and supervises broker-dealers |
| SIPC | Protects customers when a brokerage firm fails, up to $500,000 including $250,000 cash. It does not cover market losses. |

## Orders, margin, and short sales

A market order executes right away at the best available price. A limit order sets the worst price the investor will accept and may not execute. A stop order becomes a market order when the stock reaches a trigger price, which is often used to limit losses. A stop-limit order becomes a limit order at the trigger.

Buying on margin means borrowing part of the purchase price from the broker. Under Federal Reserve Regulation T, the initial margin is 50%. FINRA requires equity of at least 25% of the account's market value, and many brokers require more. If equity falls below the maintenance level, the broker issues a margin call. Margin magnifies both gains and losses.

A short sale means borrowing shares, selling them, and buying them back later to return them. The short seller profits if the price falls. Because a price can rise without limit, potential loss is unlimited. The short seller must also pay any dividends to the lender.

> **Example**
>
> An investor buys 200 shares at $50 using 50% margin. Maintenance margin is 25%. At what price does a margin call occur?
>
> 1. Purchase: 200 x 50 = $10,000. Loan: $5,000. Equity: $5,000.
> 2. A call occurs when (value - loan) / value = 0.25, which means value = loan / 0.75.
> 3. Value at call: 5,000 / 0.75 = $6,666.67. Price: 6,666.67 / 200 = $33.33.
>
> A drop to about $33.33, a 33% decline, triggers a margin call. At that price the investor has lost $3,334 of the original $5,000 equity, a 67% loss.

## Analyze a stock

Fundamental analysis estimates a company's value from its financial statements, industry, and management. It uses the annual report, the prospectus, and ratios. Technical analysis studies price and volume patterns, such as moving averages, support and resistance levels, and trends, assuming patterns reveal investor behavior.

Earnings per share (EPS) equals net income minus preferred dividends, divided by weighted common shares. The price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio equals price divided by EPS. Dividend yield equals annual dividends per share divided by price. Price-to-book equals price divided by book value per share. A high P/E can mean investors expect growth, or that the stock is expensive. Compare it with the company's history and its competitors.

> **Example**
>
> A fictional company earns $12 million with no preferred stock and has 4 million shares. The stock trades at $60 and pays $1.20 in yearly dividends.
>
> EPS is 12 / 4 = $3.00. P/E is 60 / 3 = 20. Dividend yield is 1.20 / 60 = 2%. Payout ratio is 1.20 / 3.00 = 40% of earnings.

## Market timing, the economy, and the industry

Market timing tries to move in and out of the market ahead of price changes. Research generally shows that missing a few of the best days sharply reduces long-term returns, and those days are hard to predict. Dollar-cost averaging invests a fixed amount at regular intervals, so more shares are bought when prices are low.

The economy affects securities. Rising interest rates lower bond prices and often pressure stock prices. Inflation reduces the real value of fixed payments. Recessions lower earnings, especially for cyclical companies. Industry consolidation has reduced the number of exchanges and brokerage firms, and electronic trading has lowered commissions.

## Key terms

**Underwriting.** An investment bank's process of buying or selling a new securities issue for the issuer.

**Limit order.** An order to buy or sell only at a stated price or better.

**Margin call.** A broker's demand for more money or securities when account equity falls below the maintenance requirement.

**Short sale.** Selling borrowed shares in the hope of buying them back at a lower price.

**Price-to-earnings ratio.** Share price divided by earnings per share.
