## Stocks and bonds

Common stock gives ownership and usually voting rights. Shareholders may receive dividends and gain from price increases, but they are paid last if the company fails. Preferred stock usually pays a fixed dividend, has priority over common stock for dividends and in liquidation, and usually has no vote.

A bond is a loan to the issuer. It has a par value, usually $1,000, a coupon rate that sets the yearly interest, and a maturity date. Bond prices move opposite to market interest rates. If rates rise above a bond's coupon, buyers will pay less than par for it, so it sells at a discount. If rates fall, it sells at a premium. Longer maturities change price more when rates move.

Treasury bills, notes, and bonds are backed by the U.S. government; TIPS adjust principal for inflation. Municipal bonds are issued by state and local governments and their interest is usually exempt from federal income tax. Corporate bonds carry credit risk, rated by agencies such as Moody's and S&P. Bonds rated below investment grade are called high-yield or junk bonds.

> **Example**
>
> A $1,000 bond pays a 5% coupon. It trades at $950.
>
> Yearly interest is 1,000 x 5% = $50. Current yield is 50 / 950 = 5.26%. The bond trades below par because market rates are higher than 5%. If held to maturity, the investor also gains $50 when the bond repays $1,000, so the yield to maturity is higher than the current yield.

## Funds, derivatives, and alternatives

Net asset value equals fund assets minus liabilities, divided by shares outstanding. A fund with $50 million of assets, $2 million of liabilities, and 4 million shares has an NAV of $12.

| Product | How it works | Key risk or feature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mutual fund | Pools money; priced once a day at net asset value (NAV) | Expense ratio and any sales loads reduce returns |
| Exchange-traded fund (ETF) | Fund that trades on an exchange all day | Often low-cost index tracking |
| Index fund | Holds the securities in a market index | Matches the market instead of trying to beat it |
| Call option | Right to buy at the strike price before expiration | Buyer can lose the whole premium |
| Put option | Right to sell at the strike price | Used to hedge against a price drop |
| Futures contract | Obligation to buy or sell a commodity or asset at a set price on a future date | Leverage; used by farmers and manufacturers to hedge |
| Real estate investment trust (REIT) | Company owning income-producing property; must distribute at least 90% of taxable income | Sensitive to interest rates |
| Annuity | Insurance contract that can provide income for life | Fees and surrender charges; fixed, variable, and indexed types |
| Hedge fund | Private fund using varied strategies, often with leverage | Generally limited to accredited investors; less disclosure |
| Venture capital | Investment in young private companies | High risk, illiquid, long time horizon |

## Measure performance and the cost of fees

Holding period return equals the ending value minus the beginning value plus any income, divided by the beginning value. Buying a stock at $40, collecting $2 in dividends, and selling at $45 gives (45 - 40 + 2) / 40 = 17.5%. To compare investments held for different lengths of time, convert to an annual rate.

Fees compound just like returns. Over long periods, a small difference in yearly cost creates a large difference in ending value.

Risk is often measured by standard deviation, which shows how widely returns vary, and by beta, which compares a stock's movement with the market. A beta of 1.3 means the stock tends to move 30% more than the market. Diversification reduces unsystematic risk, which is specific to one company. It cannot remove systematic risk, which affects the whole market.

> **Example**
>
> Compare $10,000 invested for 30 years earning 7% a year before fees in two funds: one charging 0.05% and one charging 1.05% a year. Round the net returns to 7% and 6% for simplicity.
>
> 1. Low-cost fund: 10,000 x 1.07^30 = 10,000 x 7.612 = $76,123.
> 2. High-cost fund: 10,000 x 1.06^30 = 10,000 x 5.743 = $57,435.
>
> The one-point fee difference costs about $18,700, which is nearly twice the original investment.

## Objectives, accounts, and advisers

An investor's objectives include growth, income, preservation of capital, and liquidity. Time horizon and risk tolerance guide asset allocation, which is the split among stocks, bonds, and cash. A common strategy is to hold more stocks when the goal is decades away and gradually shift toward bonds as the goal approaches. Rebalancing restores the chosen mix after market moves.

Tax-advantaged accounts help with long-term goals. A traditional 401(k) or 403(b) uses pre-tax contributions through an employer, often with a match. A traditional IRA may give a tax deduction now, with withdrawals taxed later. A Roth IRA uses after-tax contributions, and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. A 529 plan grows tax-free when used for qualified education expenses. Contribution limits change yearly, so check the IRS for the current year.

When choosing a professional, check FINRA BrokerCheck for licenses and disciplinary history. Broker-dealers must act in the customer's best interest when recommending investments under the SEC's Regulation Best Interest. Registered investment advisers owe a fiduciary duty. Ask how the professional is paid. Investors are responsible for reading disclosures, understanding what they buy, and reporting suspicious activity.

## Key terms

**Coupon rate.** The yearly interest a bond pays, stated as a percent of par value.

**Current yield.** A bond's yearly interest divided by its market price.

**Net asset value.** A fund's assets minus liabilities, divided by shares outstanding.

**Expense ratio.** A fund's yearly operating costs as a percent of assets.

**Systematic risk.** Market-wide risk that diversification cannot remove.
