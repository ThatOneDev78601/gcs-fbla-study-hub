## Inquiries and policy explanations

Most customer contacts are questions, not complaints. Answer with accurate information, in plain language, and confirm the customer understood. When explaining a policy the customer dislikes, give the reason briefly, then focus on what you can do: "Opened software cannot be returned because it can be copied. I can help you install it or contact the publisher's support line now."

Accessible communication meets customers where they are: speak clearly and face the customer for people who lip-read, offer written or large-print information, welcome service animals, accept relay calls, and use plain words. Cultural differences affect eye contact, personal space, directness, and how people express dissatisfaction, so watch and adapt rather than assume. Unspoken needs show up in context: a customer comparing two car seats while holding a toddler may need help with installation and time, not a faster pitch.

## Resolve complaints step by step

Empowerment means employees can offer remedies, such as a refund up to $50 or a free replacement, without asking a manager. Escalate when the remedy exceeds your authority, the customer asks for a supervisor, safety or legal issues arise, or the conversation becomes abusive. When escalating, brief the next person so the customer does not repeat the story. Follow up after the fix. A well-handled problem can leave a customer more loyal than one who never had a problem.

1. Hear: let the customer explain fully without interrupting.
2. Empathize: show you understand the impact ("Missing the delivery before your daughter's party must have been stressful").
3. Apologize sincerely for the experience, even before fault is known.
4. Resolve: offer a remedy within your authority, or explain exactly who will decide and when.
5. Diagnose and document: record the facts, remedy, and cause so the problem can be fixed for others.

> **Example**
>
> A fictional furniture store delivered a table with a scratched top. The customer calls angry. The associate may offer up to a 15% credit or arrange a replacement.
>
> The associate listens, apologizes, and paraphrases the problem. They offer two options within authority: a replacement delivered on the next available date, or a 15% credit if the customer keeps the table. The customer wants the replacement plus compensation for taking another day off work, which exceeds authority, so the associate escalates to the manager with a summary, tells the customer the manager will call by 3 p.m., and records the call. The next day the associate checks that the call happened.

## CRM records, privacy, and measurement

Customer relationship management (CRM) software stores contact information, purchase history, service cases, and preferences, so anyone helping the customer sees the history. Good records are accurate, factual, and professional, because customers can often request their data. Permissions limit who can see sensitive fields such as payment details. Collect only what the business needs, honor opt-outs, and never look up records out of curiosity.

Common service measures include customer satisfaction (CSAT) surveys, first-contact resolution rate, average response time, and Net Promoter Score (NPS). NPS asks how likely a customer is to recommend the business on a 0 to 10 scale. Scores of 9 or 10 are promoters; 0 to 6 are detractors. NPS equals the percent of promoters minus the percent of detractors.

> **Example**
>
> 200 customers answer the NPS question: 110 give 9 or 10, 50 give 7 or 8, and 40 give 0 to 6.
>
> Promoters: 110 / 200 = 55%. Detractors: 40 / 200 = 20%. NPS = 55 - 20 = 35. The 50 passives count in the total but not in either group.

## Selling and the brand promise

A brand promise is what customers expect every time, such as "fast and friendly" or "expert advice." Service delivers it or breaks it. Selling in a service role means discovering needs with questions, recommending what truly fits, demonstrating value, answering objections honestly, and closing by asking for the decision. Recommending an add-on that solves a real problem is service; pushing one that does not is not.

Emotional intelligence underlies all of it: staying calm under pressure, reading the customer's mood, and adjusting tone. Ethical choices include never promising what the company cannot deliver and treating every customer by the same standards.

>

## Key terms

**Empowerment.** Authority given to employees to resolve customer problems without approval.

**Escalation.** Passing an issue to someone with more authority or expertise.

**Net Promoter Score.** Percent of promoters minus percent of detractors on a 0 to 10 recommendation scale.

**First-contact resolution.** The share of issues solved during the customer's first contact.

**Brand promise.** The experience a brand commits to deliver every time.
