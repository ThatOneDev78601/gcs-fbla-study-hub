## Organizations and the continuum of care

Healthcare organizations include hospitals, outpatient clinics, long-term care facilities, public health agencies and other services. Primary care addresses common needs and ongoing coordination; specialty care focuses on a particular condition or field. Preventive care aims to reduce disease or detect problems earlier. Care transitions move a person between settings and can create information gaps. Administrators coordinate staffing, facilities, scheduling and resources so clinical professionals can deliver care. Administrative decisions affect patient experience and safety, but an administrator should not substitute unqualified judgment for licensed clinical decisions. Different ownership structures can influence governance and funding without eliminating responsibility for quality.

## People, strategy and communication

Workforce planning matches skills, credentials and staffing to service demand. Orientation, continuing training, supervision and clear roles support safe practice. Recruitment alone cannot solve retention problems caused by poor scheduling or an unsafe workplace. Strategic planning links community needs and organizational capacity to measurable priorities. Communicate changes with patients and staff using accessible language; confirm understanding rather than assuming a message was received. Standardized handoffs can reduce missing information at transitions. Effective leadership encourages staff to report near misses so the organization can learn before a preventable harm occurs.

> **Work through an example**
>
> A clinic has long waits even though daily visit totals are stable.
>
> Map arrivals, check-in, rooming and clinician availability by time of day. The bottleneck may reflect scheduling and uneven capacity rather than too few total staff hours.

## Structures, strategy and technology

Functional structures group people by specialty; matrix structures combine reporting relationships; multidivisional structures separate major services or locations. A chief medical officer leads medical matters, a chief nursing officer nursing practice and a chief financial officer financial stewardship. SWOT compares internal strengths and weaknesses with external opportunities and threats; PEST examines political, economic, social and technological conditions. Telehealth enables remote services but still needs suitable access, privacy and clinical judgment. Clinical information systems support care, while PACS stores and retrieves medical images. Fee-for-service pays for individual services; value-based models connect payment to defined quality and cost goals. HMOs generally emphasize network-based coordinated care, PPOs usually offer more out-of-network flexibility and POS plans combine features; actual terms vary.

## Quality and process improvement

Quality includes safety, effectiveness, timeliness, patient-centeredness, efficiency and equity. Measure the process before changing it, choose an intervention and check the result. A root-cause analysis examines system contributors rather than stopping at 'someone made a mistake.' Track both intended outcomes and balancing measures, such as whether shorter visits increase callbacks. Patient satisfaction is useful but cannot alone establish clinical quality. Common mistakes include treating every delay as a staffing shortage, using averages that hide underserved groups and discouraging reporting to make incident counts appear low. Support a culture that distinguishes learning from concealment.

## Key terms to remember

**Continuum of care.** Connected services across a person’s different health needs and settings.

**Near miss.** An event that could have caused harm but did not.

**Bottleneck.** The step whose limited capacity constrains a process.
