## A routine for the preparation time

Impromptu events give a topic and a short preparation period, then a speech of a few minutes. Check the current event guide for the exact preparation time, speaking time, and permitted materials, such as whether a note card is allowed. A fixed routine prevents panic, because you already know what to do with every minute.

1. Read the prompt twice and underline the key words. Decide what it is really asking.
2. Choose one clear theme or answer in a single sentence. Take a position rather than listing every side.
3. Pick a structure from your memorized set.
4. Choose two or three examples: one personal, one from business or history, one from current events or a book you know well.
5. Plan an opening line and a closing line that echo each other.
6. Use any remaining time to rehearse the opening in your head.

## Structures to memorize

Practice each structure until you can outline a speech in under a minute. Keep a mental bank of 8 to 10 flexible examples: a personal challenge, a team experience, a well-known business turnaround, an inventor, a historical leader, and a recent local event. Most prompts can connect to several of them.

| Structure | Parts | Fits prompts like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PREP | Point, reason, example, point restated | Opinion questions |
| Past, present, future | How it was, how it is, how it should be | Change and trends |
| Problem, cause, solution | What is wrong, why, what to do | Challenges |
| Two sides, then verdict | One view, the other view, your conclusion | Debatable questions |
| Three lessons | Three takeaways from one story | Quotations and values |

## Annotated practice responses

Practice prompt: "Is failure a better teacher than success?" The outline below uses two sides, then a verdict.

> **Example**
>
> Outline a 3-minute response to "Is failure a better teacher than success?"
>
> 1. Opening: "The first time I ran a bake sale, we sold out in an hour, and I learned almost nothing. The second time, we lost money, and I learned everything." (A personal story that previews the theme.)
> 2. Thesis: "Failure teaches more, but only if we study it."
> 3. Side one: success shows what works, using a company that copied its own winning product line.
> 4. Side two: failure forces analysis, using the inventor who tested hundreds of materials before finding one that worked.
> 5. Verdict: failure teaches when we ask why; give two questions to ask after any setback.
> 6. Closing: return to the bake sale: "The money we lost that day was the cheapest business class I ever took."
>
> The outline works because it takes a clear position, uses different kinds of examples, and ends by echoing the opening story. Five or six key words on a note card are enough to deliver it.

## Delivery from notes

Speak from key words, not sentences, and look at the audience, not the card. Use plain language; complex vocabulary under pressure leads to stumbles. Pause instead of saying "um." Use transitions to show structure aloud: "On the other hand," "That brings me to my verdict." Watch the time signals and finish the conclusion even if you must shorten a point.

Practice with new prompts several times a week, timing both preparation and delivery. Write your own prompts or trade them with a partner so they are truly unseen.

> **Try it yourself**
>
> Set a timer for your event's preparation time and outline a response to this prompt: "What makes a good team member?" Use PREP or three lessons, two different examples, and an opening that the closing echoes. Then deliver it aloud and time it.

## Key terms

**Impromptu speech.** A speech delivered with little preparation on an unseen topic.

**PREP structure.** Point, reason, example, and point restated.

**Example bank.** A set of prepared, flexible stories and facts that can fit many prompts.

**Echo close.** A conclusion that returns to the speech's opening image or line.
