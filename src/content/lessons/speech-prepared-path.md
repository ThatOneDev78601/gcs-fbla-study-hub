## Research and choose a thesis

Begin by reading the current topic carefully and listing what it asks. Brainstorm angles, then research the strongest with credible sources: studies, books, reputable news, and interviews. For a topic about leadership habits or service, look for specific, verifiable examples and research findings rather than general quotations. Record each fact with its source so you can cite it aloud.

A thesis is one sentence stating your main claim, such as "Curiosity is a skill we can practice, not a trait we are born with." This lesson uses that unrelated practice topic; students research and write their own competition speeches. Choose two or three main points that each support the thesis with evidence and examples.

## Organize for listeners

The introduction gets attention (a story, a surprising fact, or a question), states the thesis, and previews the points. Transitions signal movement: "That explains why curiosity matters. Now, how do we practice it?" The conclusion signals the end, summarizes the points, and closes with a memorable line that ties back to the opening.

| Pattern | Best for | Example points (practice topic: curiosity) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Topical | Separate aspects of a topic | Curiosity at school, at work, in relationships |
| Problem-solution | Persuasion | Problem: students stop asking questions; solution: three habits |
| Chronological | Change over time | Childhood questions, school years, adult careers |
| Cause-effect | Explaining results | Why curiosity declines and what that costs |
| Monroe's motivated sequence | Calls to action | Attention, need, satisfaction, visualization, action |

## Write for the ear and revise

A speech is not an essay read aloud. Use shorter sentences, everyday words, and repetition of key phrases so listeners can follow without rereading. Signposts ("first," "the second habit is") act as headings. Groups of three are easy to remember. Replace abstract claims with concrete examples and stories.

Cite sources orally: "According to a 2023 survey by the Pew Research Center..." Revise for accuracy (check every number and quotation), audience fit (would these judges understand every term?), and support (does every point have evidence?). Cut anything that does not serve the thesis. Read the draft aloud and mark where you stumble.

> **Example**
>
> Revise this essay sentence for speaking: "Notwithstanding the prevalence of standardized curricula, the cultivation of inquisitiveness remains an underemphasized pedagogical objective."
>
> "Schools teach a lot of answers. They don't always teach us how to ask good questions." The revision splits one dense sentence into two short ones, uses everyday words, and creates a contrast listeners can hear.

## Rehearse delivery within the rules

Most speakers talk at about 130 to 150 words per minute in a speech, so a 4-minute speech is roughly 520 to 600 words. Time several full rehearsals and adjust content, not speed. Check the current event rules for time limits, penalties, and whether notes are allowed; if allowed, use brief keyword notes, not a script.

Delivery includes vocal variety (rate, pitch, volume), purposeful pauses before key points, eye contact spread across the room, natural gestures that match your words, steady posture, and movement only with a purpose, such as stepping forward at a transition. Record yourself and watch for filler words and repeated habits. Rehearse in clothes and on a stage-like space if you can.

> **Try it yourself**
>
> Score a recorded rehearsal of your speech, or a 2-minute practice speech on "a skill worth practicing," using these criteria from 1 to 5: clear thesis, organized points with transitions, supporting evidence, vocal variety, eye contact, gestures, and timing. Write one specific change for your next rehearsal.

## Key terms

**Thesis.** A one-sentence statement of a speech's main claim.

**Signpost.** A word or phrase that tells listeners where the speech is going.

**Monroe's motivated sequence.** A persuasive pattern: attention, need, satisfaction, visualization, and action.

**Vocal variety.** Changes in rate, pitch, and volume that add meaning and interest.

**Oral citation.** Naming a source aloud during a speech.
