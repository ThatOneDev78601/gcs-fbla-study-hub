## Write a measurable objective

A learning objective states what learners will be able to do by the end. The ABCD model includes the audience, the behavior (an observable verb), the condition, and the degree of success: "Given a monthly allowance and expense list (condition), seventh graders (audience) will build a balanced budget (behavior) with no math errors (degree)." Avoid verbs that cannot be observed, such as "understand" or "appreciate."

Bloom's revised taxonomy orders thinking from remember, understand, and apply to analyze, evaluate, and create. Match verbs to the level you intend: "list" is remembering; "compare" is analyzing; "design" is creating. A short lesson usually targets one main objective.

This lesson's examples use budgeting for middle school students. Competitors plan their own lesson on the assigned leadership topic, with content accurate and suitable for the assigned age group. Leadership skills such as communication, goal setting, teamwork, and responsibility can be taught the same way: define the skill, model it, practice it, and check it.

## Plan the sequence

List materials and their preparation, and time every part. Plan access needs using Universal Design for Learning: present information in more than one way (spoken, visual, written), offer more than one way to show learning, and build in choice and relevance to keep learners engaged. Plan for learners who finish early and for those who need extra support.

1. Hook and prior knowledge (3 minutes): ask a question that connects to learners' lives, such as "What would you do with $50 a month?" A quick KWL (know, want to know, learned) chart surfaces what they already know and their misconceptions.
2. Direct instruction and modeling, "I do" (5 minutes): explain the idea briefly and think aloud through one example.
3. Guided practice, "we do" (7 minutes): solve a second example together, asking learners to suggest each step.
4. Independent practice, "you do" (8 minutes): learners complete a task alone or in pairs while the teacher circulates.
5. Check and close (5 minutes): an exit ticket question that directly measures the objective, then a summary.

## Check for understanding and handle misconceptions

Check understanding often, not only at the end. Quick checks include asking every learner to hold up a number of fingers for an answer, mini whiteboards, think-pair-share, and targeted questions with three to five seconds of wait time. Ask "why" follow-ups so you hear reasoning, not just answers.

Formative assessment happens during learning to guide teaching; summative assessment measures learning at the end. Anticipate common misconceptions and plan how to address them. In budgeting, students often treat savings as whatever is left over instead of a planned category; a contrasting example shows the difference.

> **Example**
>
> During guided practice, half the class lists a phone bill as a "want."
>
> Pause and ask, "What happens if the phone bill isn't paid?" Discuss how needs and wants depend on the situation, then give a revised rule: a need is required for safety, health, school, or work obligations. Re-check with two new items before moving on. The teacher records this misconception to address earlier in the next version of the lesson.

## Teach live and revise

In a live demonstration, judges may act as learners. Teach them as you would the intended age group: give clear directions, use the materials, ask real questions, and respond to their answers. Choose the section of the plan that best shows interaction, such as guided practice, rather than only lecturing. Keep to time and finish with a check that shows whether the objective was met.

After teaching, evaluate with evidence: what percentage of exit tickets met the objective, which mistakes repeated, and which activity took longer than planned. Revise the plan accordingly and note what changed. Score your plan against the event's lesson-plan rating criteria before submitting.

> **Try it yourself**
>
> Write an ABCD objective, a 20-minute sequence with times, and one exit ticket question for an unrelated topic: teaching fifth graders to write a polite email request.

## Key terms

**Learning objective.** A statement of what learners will be able to do after instruction.

**Bloom's taxonomy.** A hierarchy of thinking levels from remembering to creating.

**Gradual release.** Moving from teacher modeling to guided practice to independent work.

**Formative assessment.** Checks during learning used to adjust instruction.

**Exit ticket.** A short task at the end of a lesson that shows whether learners met the objective.

**Universal Design for Learning.** Planning lessons with multiple ways to represent content, engage learners, and show learning.
