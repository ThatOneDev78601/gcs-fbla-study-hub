## Preproduction

Most video problems are solved before the camera rolls. Start with the audience, the message, and the required length. Write a script or outline, then a storyboard: a sequence of simple sketches showing each shot with notes on dialogue, sound, and duration. Turn it into a shot list grouped by location so you film efficiently, and a schedule with people, places, equipment, and backup dates.

Get signed releases from everyone who appears on camera (a parent signs for minors) and permission for private locations. Plan the sound: will you use a voice-over, interviews, music, or natural sound?

## Shots, framing, and camera settings

Use the rule of thirds, leave headroom above heads, and give lead room in the direction a person looks or moves. The 180-degree rule keeps the camera on one side of an imaginary line between two people so their screen directions stay consistent. Use a tripod or stabilizer, and shoot extra footage before and after each action for editing.

Choose a frame rate and keep it consistent: 24 frames per second looks like film, 30 is common for video, and 60 allows smooth slow motion. A shutter speed about double the frame rate (1/50 for 24 or 25 fps, 1/60 for 30 fps) gives natural motion blur. Keep ISO low to avoid noise, and set white balance so white looks white under the current light.

| Shot | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Wide or establishing shot | Shows the location and context |
| Medium shot | Waist up; conversation and action |
| Close-up | Face or detail; emotion and emphasis |
| Extreme close-up | A small detail such as hands on a keyboard |
| Over-the-shoulder | Conversations between two people |
| Point of view | What a character sees |

## Light and sound

Three-point lighting uses a key light (main light, to one side of the camera), a fill light (softer, on the other side, to reduce shadows), and a back light (behind the subject, to separate them from the background). A window can serve as a key light; a white poster board can bounce light as fill. Avoid placing the subject in front of a bright window.

Viewers forgive imperfect video more than bad sound. Use an external microphone close to the speaker: a lavalier clips to clothing, a shotgun mic points at the subject from just outside the frame, and a handheld suits interviews in noisy places. Wear headphones while recording. Set levels so voices peak around -12 to -6 dBFS; sound that hits 0 clips and distorts permanently. Record 30 seconds of room tone, the quiet sound of the space, to smooth edits.

> **Example**
>
> Common faults and fixes: (a) the interview audio echoes; (b) the subject's face is dark; (c) the footage looks orange; (d) the speaker's voice is distorted on loud words.
>
> (a) Move to a room with soft furnishings, move the mic closer, or hang blankets. (b) Turn the subject toward the window or add a key light; do not shoot with a bright window behind them. (c) Set white balance for the light source or adjust it in editing. (d) Audio clipped above 0 dBFS; lower the gain and rerecord, since clipping cannot be fully repaired.

## Edit, caption, and export

Pacing matches the mood: quick cuts build energy, longer shots allow reflection. Captions should be accurate, synced, and readable, and they are essential for viewers who are deaf or hard of hearing and for people watching with the sound off.

1. Organize footage into folders by scene and rename clips.
2. Assemble a rough cut in story order, focusing on structure, not polish.
3. Refine the fine cut: trim pauses, vary shot sizes, and use cutaways (B-roll) to hide jump cuts.
4. Use J-cuts (audio of the next shot starts early) and L-cuts (audio continues into the next shot) for natural transitions. Use simple cuts most of the time; flashy transitions distract.
5. Mix sound: dialogue clear and consistent, music lower under speech, no sudden jumps in volume.
6. Color-correct so shots match, then add titles, lower thirds, captions, and credits.
7. Export using the required settings, often an MP4 file with H.264 video at 1920 x 1080 and the same frame rate you shot. Watch the whole export before submitting.

## Permissions, sources, and production records

Use only music, footage, images, and fonts you created or have permission to use. "Royalty-free" means no per-use fee, not free of a license. Creative Commons licenses state conditions: BY requires credit, SA requires sharing under the same license, NC bans commercial use, and ND bans changes. Some events ban previously published video clips entirely, so check the guide.

Keep a production record: the script and storyboard versions, shot list, release forms, a source log for every asset, the equipment and software used, and notes on problems and fixes. Judges often ask how you made specific choices, and the record lets you answer precisely.

## Key terms

**Storyboard.** A sequence of sketches showing each planned shot.

**Shot list.** An organized list of every shot needed, often grouped by location.

**180-degree rule.** Keeping the camera on one side of an imaginary line between subjects to preserve screen direction.

**Three-point lighting.** A setup using key, fill, and back lights.

**Clipping.** Distortion caused when audio exceeds the maximum recording level.

**J-cut.** An edit where the next shot's audio begins before its video.
