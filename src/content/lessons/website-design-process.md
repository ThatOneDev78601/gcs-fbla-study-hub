## Research and plan content

Start with the people the site serves and the tasks they come to do. Research through short interviews or surveys and by studying similar sites. Write two or three user tasks, such as "Find this week's volunteer opportunities and sign up in under two minutes." These tasks guide every design decision and become your test plan. This lesson uses a fictional community garden site, unrelated to the competition topic.

Make a content inventory listing every piece of content needed, who provides it, and where it lives. Group content by what users look for, not by how the organization is structured. Draw a sitemap showing pages and their hierarchy, and keep important pages no more than two or three clicks from home.

## Wireframes and visual design

Wireframes are simple layouts showing where content and controls go, without colors or images. Start with low-fidelity sketches for each page type, including the phone layout, then make higher-fidelity mockups. Get feedback on wireframes, when changes are cheap.

Visual design applies hierarchy, a consistent grid, a limited color palette with accessible contrast, and one or two typefaces. Keep navigation, headers, and footers identical on every page. Write in plain language with short paragraphs and descriptive headings and link text ("View the planting calendar" instead of "Click here"). Cite sources for facts and credit images.

> **Example**
>
> Explain two design decisions for the community garden home page.
>
> Decision 1: the "Volunteer this week" button sits at the top of the home page in the accent color, because interviews showed volunteering was the most common reason people visit. Decision 2: the phone layout stacks the calendar below the button and hides secondary links in a labeled menu, because four of five testers used phones. Each decision cites research and a user task.

## Build and add interactive elements

Build in a tool the current event guide permits, and confirm whether site builders, templates, or hand-coded pages are allowed. Create the required pages and working interactive elements such as forms, filters, maps, or calendars. Every interactive element needs clear labels, keyboard access, visible feedback, and helpful error messages. Include a friendly page for broken links (a 404 page) that offers a way back.

## Test, measure, and present

Test on at least three device categories, such as desktop, tablet, and phone, and in more than one browser. Check readability, layout, links, forms, and loading speed. Run usability tests: give five people your user tasks, watch without helping, and record whether they succeed, how long it takes, and where they hesitate. Keep a testing record with dates, results, and the revision made for each problem.

Define success measures before launch, such as task completion rate, time on task, form submissions, or the share of visitors who reach the volunteer page. Present the site by walking through the research, planning artifacts (sitemap and wireframes), key design decisions and their reasons, implementation, testing results and revisions, and how success would be measured.

> **Try it yourself**
>
> Write a usability test plan for one task on a site you could design: the task wording, the success criteria, what you will observe, and how you will record results.

## Key terms

**Content inventory.** A list of all content a site needs, with owners and locations.

**Sitemap.** A diagram of a website's pages and their hierarchy.

**Wireframe.** A simple layout showing where content and controls go, without visual styling.

**Usability test.** Observing real users attempting tasks to find design problems.

**Task completion rate.** The share of users who complete a task successfully.
